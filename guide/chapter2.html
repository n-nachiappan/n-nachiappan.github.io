IRAs: Traditional and Spousal

Summary

  • An IRA (Individual Retirement Arrangement) is a retirement account you open yourself, independent of any employer — you control the provider, the investments, and the fees.
  • For 2026 you can contribute up to $7,500 across all your IRAs, plus a $1,100 catch-up at 50+. You need earned income at least equal to what you contribute.
  • A traditional IRA may be tax-deductible now — but if you (or your spouse) are covered by a workplace plan, the deduction phases out at higher incomes. Above those limits you can still contribute non-deductibly (which sets up the backdoor Roth in Chapter 3).
  • The spousal IRA is the key rule most couples miss: a spouse with little or no income can still fund a full IRA, using the working spouse's earned income, as long as you file jointly.
  • Compared with a 401(k), an IRA has a lower contribution limit but far more freedom — any brokerage, any fund, usually lower costs.
  • IRAs are where old 401(k)s often land after you change jobs (a "rollover IRA"), and where much of retirement-account wealth eventually lives.

What an IRA is. Unlike a 401(k), which your employer sponsors, an IRA is opened by you at a bank or brokerage. That independence is its advantage: you're not stuck with a limited menu of funds or a plan's fee structure — you can hold nearly any stock, bond, ETF, or mutual fund, and shop for the lowest costs. The tradeoff is a smaller annual limit.

The 2026 limits and the earned-income rule. You can contribute up to $7,500 total across all your IRAs (traditional and Roth combined), plus $1,100 more if you're 50 or older — so $8,600 for older savers. One firm requirement: you must have earned income (wages or self-employment income) at least equal to your contribution. Investment income, Social Security, and pensions don't count. This is where the spousal rule becomes essential.

Traditional IRA deductibility. Contributions to a traditional IRA may be deductible, but the rules depend on whether you're covered by a workplace plan: - If neither you nor your spouse is covered by a workplace plan, your traditional IRA contribution is fully deductible regardless of income. - If you are covered by a workplace plan, the deduction phases out for 2026 between $81,000–$91,000 of income (single) and $129,000–$149,000 (married filing jointly). - If your spouse is covered but you aren't, your deduction phases out between $242,000–$252,000 (joint).

Above these ranges you can still make a traditional IRA contribution — it's just non-deductible. Non-deductible contributions create "basis" you track on Form 8606 so you're not taxed twice later, and they're the raw material for the backdoor Roth (Chapter 3).

The spousal IRA — a full account for the non-earner. Normally you need your own earned income to fund an IRA. The spousal IRA is the exception, and it's genuinely valuable: if you file jointly and one spouse has enough earned income, the other spouse can fund a full IRA of their own — up to the same $7,500 (+$1,100 catch-up) — even with zero personal income. The contribution goes into an account in the non-earning spouse's own name (it's still individual). The only limit is that the working spouse's earned income must cover both contributions combined.

Worked example. Maria earns $120,000; her husband David is a stay-at-home parent with no wages. Without the spousal rule, David couldn't fund an IRA at all. With it, and because they file jointly, David opens his own IRA and contributes the full $7,500 (funded from household income). The couple now shelters $15,000 across two IRAs instead of $7,500 — doubling their IRA savings and building retirement assets in both names, which matters for tax diversification and for the survivor down the road.

Traditional IRA vs. 401(k) — when each shines. They're complements, not rivals. The 401(k) has a much higher limit ($24,500 vs. $7,500) and often an employer match, so it's usually the first priority up to the match. The IRA's edge is choice and cost — no plan menu, no plan fees. A common sequence: fund the 401(k) to the match, then max an IRA (often a Roth, Chapter 3), then return to the 401(k) for more.

The rollover IRA. When you leave a job, rolling an old 401(k) into an IRA is common — it consolidates accounts and usually cuts fees. Just remember the two cautions from elsewhere in this book: rolling to an IRA can forfeit the Rule of 55 (Chapter 19), and a large pre-tax IRA balance can complicate a future backdoor Roth via the pro-rata rule (Chapter 3). Roll with intent, not by reflex.

Key action items

  • Open and fund an IRA — for both spouses. If you're married and file jointly, use the spousal IRA so a non-earning spouse gets a full account too.
  • Check your deduction eligibility. If a workplace plan covers you, see where your income falls in the phase-out before assuming the contribution is deductible.
  • Track non-deductible basis on Form 8606. If you contribute above the deduction limits, file it every year so you're not taxed twice.
  • Sequence with your 401(k). Match first, then IRA, then more 401(k) — adjust for your fee and fund options.
  • Consolidate old 401(k)s thoughtfully. A rollover IRA simplifies life, but weigh the Rule-of-55 and pro-rata implications first.
