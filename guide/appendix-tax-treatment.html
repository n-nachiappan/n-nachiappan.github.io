Reference · Appendix D

Tax Treatment by Account Type

The idea

  • Every account gets taxed — or spared — at up to three moments: when money goes in (contribution), while it grows, and when it comes out (withdrawal). A fourth moment, what happens at death, decides what your heirs actually keep. This appendix lines all four up side by side.
  • There are really only four deals in the code: pre-tax (traditional) pays tax at the end, Roth pays up front, the HSA (for medical costs) never pays at all, and the taxable account pays a little all along — but at gentle rates. Knowing which moment each one taxes is retirement tax planning.
  • The rules below are scattered across the book by design — each account earns its own chapter. This page is the one-screen version of Chapters 1–6 (building the buckets), 10 and 17–18 (spending them), and 26 (passing them on).
  • Figures are 2026. Pair this with Appendix C for the bracket tables the "taxed" cells resolve to, and Appendix B for every contribution limit.

1 · Where each account is taxed — the three moments

Start with the shape. Read each row left to right as the life of a dollar: does the tax hit going in, while it grows, or coming out? The whole game is steering income toward the account whose "taxed" moment lands in your lowest-rate year.

AccountMoney going inWhile it growsMoney coming outPattern
Traditional 401(k) / IRAUntaxed (deducted now)UntaxedTaxed (ordinary)pay at the end
Roth 401(k) / IRATaxed (now)UntaxedTax-freepay up front

*The HSA is tax-free coming out only for qualified medical expenses (including Medicare Part B & D premiums). After 65, other withdrawals are allowed but taxed like a traditional account — see the detail table. "Traditional" and "Roth" behave the same whether the wrapper is a 401(k)/403(b)/457(b) or an IRA; the differences that do exist between them are in §2. And all of this is federal: California and New Jersey don't conform — they deny the contribution deduction and tax the HSA's dividends, interest, and realized gains yearly (you self-report it; no 1099 is issued), though withdrawals still escape — making it a double, not triple, advantage in those two states (Ch 19).

State-tax-free interest is the edge; TIPS/I bonds are the inflation hedge.

3 · Getting at the money — penalties, ages & RMDs

The "coming out" column above assumes you're 59½ or older and past any 5-year clock. Two conditions complicate it: the 10% early-withdrawal penalty before 59½, and required minimum distributions (RMDs) that eventually force pre-tax money out whether you need it or not.

AccountOwner RMDs?Before 59½Main early-access door
Traditional 401(k)Yes — from 73 (75 if born 1960+)10% penalty + taxRule of 55 (Ch 21); 72(t) SEPP (Ch 20)
Traditional IRAYes — from 7310% penalty + tax72(t) SEPP (Ch 20)
Roth 401(k)No (from 2024)Earnings penalized if unqualifiedRoll to a Roth IRA to reach contributions
Roth IRANo — everContributions free; earnings 10% + taxWithdraw contributions anytime; mind the 5-yr conversion clock
HSANoNon-medical = 20% penalty + taxSpend on medical — always tax-free, any age
TaxableNoNone — fully liquidSell anytime; hold > 1 yr for the long-term rate

RMDs matter for more than cash flow: the forced income can push you into a higher bracket, trigger the Social Security "tax torpedo," and cross an IRMAA line — the case for draining or converting the pre-tax bucket before 73 (Ch 12, Ch 18). QCDs (from 70½, up to $108,000) let you satisfy an RMD tax-free by giving it to charity.

4 · What your heirs inherit

The most consequential difference between these accounts shows up after you're gone. With the federal estate tax now irrelevant for nearly every family (2026 exemption: $15M per person), legacy planning is really about income-tax character and the step-up in basis (Ch 26).

AccountStep-up at death?How the heir is taxedDistribution rule (non-spouse)
Taxable brokerageYes — basis resets to date-of-death value~$0 on all pre-death gainsNone — heirs can hold indefinitely
Roth 401(k) / IRAN/A (never taxed anyway)Tax-freeEmpty within 10 years; no annual RMDs — grows tax-free meanwhile
Traditional 401(k) / IRANoOrdinary income (IRD), at the heir's rateEmpty within 10 years; + annual RMDs if you'd already started
HSANoSpouse: stays an HSA. Anyone else: whole balance = income that yearNon-spouse: immediate, in full

The punchline the guide keeps returning to: leave the step-up (taxable) and tax-free (Roth) dollars to your heirs, and spend down or convert the pre-tax accounts yourself — it's the mirror image of in-retirement sequencing (Ch 17). And name a spouse on the HSA, or spend it, so it never lands on a non-spouse as a one-year tax bomb.

Worked example

The same $10,000 of salary, four tax fates

Take $10,000 of salary, a 22% tax rate both now and in retirement, and 7% growth for 25 years (a 5.4× multiplier). Where you route it changes what you actually keep:

RouteInvested after taxGrows toTax at withdrawalNet in hand
Traditional$10,000$54,27422%$42,334
Roth$7,800$42,334$0$42,334
Taxable$7,800~$37,700*15% on gain~$33,200
HSA (medical)$10,000$54,274$0$54,274

When your tax rate is the same going in and coming out, Traditional and Roth tie exactly — $42,334 either way — so the real choice is a bet on your future bracket: Roth wins if it's higher later, Traditional if it's lower. The taxable account trails both, because you fund it with after-tax dollars and pay tax on dividends along the way. And the HSA, spent on medical care, is untaxed at all three moments — which is why the guide calls it the best account in the code.

*Approximate: a ~2% dividend yield taxed yearly drags the taxable account's compounding below 7%, and a final long-term capital-gains bill trims it again. The exact figure depends on turnover and yield.

Rules and dollar figures are for the 2026 plan year (IRS, SSA, and CMS releases; contribution limits per Notice 2025-67). "Traditional" and "Roth" cover 401(k)/403(b)/457(b)/TSP and IRA wrappers except where the table notes a difference. Contribution limits and brackets reset annually with inflation; the Roth IRA income limits and estate exemption are indexed, while a few thresholds are fixed in law — reconfirm current-year numbers before acting. Nothing here is personalized financial, tax, or legal advice; confirm your own situation with a CPA or the IRS.
