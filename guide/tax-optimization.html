Tax Optimization in Retirement

Summary

  • In retirement you gain something you never had while working: control over your taxable income — that control is the whole game.
  • Which account you pull from, and when, largely determines your tax bill.
  • The goal isn't to minimize this year's taxes — it's to minimize taxes over your whole retirement, which often means voluntarily paying a little more now (in low-income years) to avoid a lot more later.
  • Three big retiree-specific traps: the tax torpedo (how extra income makes more of your Social Security taxable), capital-gains stacking, and the interaction with Medicare's IRMAA surcharges (Chapter 11).
  • 2026 brings unusually generous deductions for seniors: on top of the standard deduction, those 65+ get an age add-on and a new temporary $6,000 bonus deduction.
  • A single 65-year-old can shelter about $24,150 of income before owing a dime of federal tax.
  • The 0% long-term capital gains bracket is a genuine gift: below a taxable-income threshold, qualified gains are taxed at zero.
  • Think in terms of filling low tax brackets in your gap years rather than avoiding all taxable income.

The new lever: income control. While working, your income is whatever your employer pays you. In retirement, you decide how much taxable income to generate by choosing which "bucket" to spend from — taxable brokerage, tax-deferred (traditional 401(k)/IRA), or tax-free (Roth). Each is taxed differently: brokerage withdrawals trigger only capital gains on the growth, traditional withdrawals are fully ordinary income, and Roth withdrawals are tax-free. Blend them deliberately and you can hit a target spending level at very different tax costs.

Play the long game, not the annual game. The instinct to minimize this year's tax is often exactly wrong. Many retirees have "gap years" — after work stops but before Social Security and RMDs begin — when taxable income is unusually low. Those years are precious: fill up the low brackets on purpose (via Roth conversions or strategic traditional withdrawals) so you don't get forced into high brackets later when RMDs and Social Security stack on top of each other. Paying 12% now to avoid 24% at 75 is a win, even though it raises this year's bill.

2026's generous senior deductions. The One Big Beautiful Bill Act (2025) reshaped the deduction landscape. For 2026 the base standard deduction is $16,100 (single) / $32,200 (married filing jointly). Those 65+ get an additional age deduction (about $2,050 single / $1,650 per married spouse). And there's a new, temporary $6,000 per-person bonus deduction for those 65+ (tax years 2025–2028), which phases out above $75,000 MAGI (single) / $150,000 (joint). Stacked together, a single 65-year-old can shield roughly $24,150, and a married couple both 65+ can shield around $47,500, before any federal income tax applies. For many middle-income retirees this effectively zeroes out federal tax on a big chunk of income — plan around it while it lasts.

The tax torpedo. Social Security benefits are taxable based on your "combined income" (AGI + tax-exempt interest + half your benefits). As that figure crosses certain thresholds, the share of your benefits subject to tax climbs from 0% toward a maximum of 85%. The nasty part: because each extra dollar of other income can also drag more of your Social Security into the taxable column, your effective marginal rate in that zone can spike well above your stated bracket — a $1 withdrawal can create far more than $1 of taxable income. Worse, the thresholds haven't been adjusted for inflation since the 1980s–90s, so more retirees hit them every year. Managing income to stay under or move cleanly through this zone is a core retirement-tax skill.

Worked example

The tax torpedo in action

Eleanor, 68 and single, receives $24,000 of Social Security and pulls $20,000 from her traditional IRA. Her "combined income" for the Social Security formula is:

$20,000 other income + ½ × $24,000 benefits = $32,000

That sits in the range where each extra dollar of income also drags 50¢ of her benefits into the taxable column. So she takes $1,000 more from her IRA:

A $1,000 withdrawal creates…Taxable income
The withdrawal itself$1,000
Extra Social Security now taxable (50¢ on the dollar)$500
New taxable income from a $1,000 draw$1,500

In the 12% bracket, that $1,500 of taxable income costs $180 — an 18% effective rate on a $1,000 withdrawal, not 12%. Deeper into the torpedo, where 85¢ of each dollar becomes taxable, the same 12% bracket bites at about 22% and a 22% bracket at about 41%. That invisible spike is why you plan withdrawals to move cleanly through this zone, not stall inside it.

The 0% capital-gains bracket. Long-term capital gains and qualified dividends get their own preferential brackets: 0%, 15%, or 20%. In 2026 a single filer pays 0% on long-term gains while taxable income stays under about $48,350 (roughly double for joint filers), 15% up to about $533,400, and 20% above. In a low-income year, you can realize gains — or do "tax-gain harvesting," selling and immediately rebuying to reset your cost basis — entirely tax-free. This is one of the most underused moves in early retirement.

Asset location. Beyond when you withdraw, where you hold assets matters. As a rule of thumb, tax-inefficient assets (bonds, REITs, actively traded funds) belong in tax-deferred/Roth accounts, while tax-efficient assets (index funds, individual stocks you'll hold long-term) belong in taxable brokerage where they enjoy preferential gains rates and a step-up in basis at death. Getting location right can add a meaningful amount over decades without changing your overall allocation.

Key action items

  • Map your three buckets. Know your taxable, tax-deferred, and tax-free balances — this is the raw material for every tax move.
  • Exploit gap years. In low-income years before RMDs/Social Security, fill low brackets with Roth conversions or gain harvesting on purpose (see Chapters 12 and 18).
  • Claim the senior deductions. If you're 65+ and under the phase-out, make sure you capture the age add-on and the $6,000 (per person) bonus deduction through 2028.
  • Respect the torpedo. Before a large withdrawal, check whether it will drag more of your Social Security into taxable income and spike your effective rate.
  • Use the 0% gains bracket. Realize long-term gains tax-free in years your taxable income sits under the threshold.
  • Fix asset location. Put bonds/REITs in tax-sheltered accounts, tax-efficient equities in taxable.
