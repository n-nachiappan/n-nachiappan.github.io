Where You'll Live: Aging in Place, Downsizing, and CCRCs

Summary

  • Where you live in later retirement shapes your cost, your care, and your connections more than almost any other decision — and "age in place" often has to become "age in the right place."
  • Long-term care is the likely, expensive backdrop to this decision — about 7 in 10 people turning 65 will need some, and Medicare doesn't cover the custodial kind. Chapter 10 works through the odds, the cost tables, LTC insurance, and Medicaid; this chapter is about where that care actually happens.
  • Housing is a continuum: aging in place (with home modifications and community support), downsizing or independent living, and continuing-care retirement communities (CCRCs) that bundle independent living, assisted living, and nursing on one campus.
  • A CCRC trades a large entrance fee (~$150k to $1M+, often partly refundable) plus a monthly fee for a locked-in continuum of care — and its four contract types (A–D) decide who bears the cost of the care you may later need.
  • The through-line: decide before a crisis forces it. The worst housing-and-care decisions are made from a hospital bed; the best are made years ahead, with the numbers run.

The decision hiding inside every long retirement. Roughly three-quarters of adults over 50 say they want to stay in their own home as long as possible — and for a while, most do. But a retirement can run thirty years, and somewhere in the back half the house that was perfect at 65 can turn into a liability: stairs you shouldn't climb, a yard you can't keep, a town you can no longer drive around, and a growing isolation as the neighborhood turns over and friends move or pass. "Aging in place" is a wonderful default right up until it quietly becomes the wrong one. This chapter is about seeing the whole continuum early, so the move — if you need it — is a choice you make, not an emergency someone makes for you.

Long-term care is the likely backdrop — and Chapter 10 has the depth. Before choosing where to live, absorb the base rate: roughly 70% of people turning 65 will need long-term care at some point, about 20% for more than five years, and Medicare does not cover the custodial kind — it pays only for short, skilled, rehabilitative stays. It's expensive, too: assisted living runs a national median around $70,800/year and a shared nursing-home room past $111,000, both rising faster than inflation. Chapter 10 lays out the full odds, the cost tables, and the four ways to fund it (self-fund, long-term-care insurance, a hybrid policy, or Medicaid after spend-down). This chapter takes the need as given and answers the question Chapter 10 doesn't: where that care actually happens — and how to choose the setting before a crisis chooses it for you.

The housing continuum. Between "the house I have now" and "a nursing home" lies a whole spectrum most people never learn until they need it:

• Aging in place, made safe. Staying put, but adapting the home (single-floor living, a no-step entry, grab bars, better lighting — "universal design") and plugging into support. The Village movement (a few hundred member-run, neighbor-helping-neighbor networks nationwide), naturally occurring "NORCs," and cohousing communities all let people stay independent with a scaffold of help and company around them.
• Downsizing / 55+ / independent living. A smaller, low-maintenance home or an age-restricted community — less house to manage, often more built-in social life, no personal care yet.
• Assisted living. Your own apartment plus help with daily activities and medications, meals, and social programming — for when independent living gets hard but nursing-level care isn't needed.
• Memory care. Assisted living specialized and secured for dementia.
• Skilled nursing. Round-the-clock medical care — the most intensive and expensive rung.

CCRCs — the whole continuum on one campus. A Continuing-Care Retirement Community (increasingly called a "Life Plan Community") bundles that entire ladder into a single place: you move in while independent, and if your needs grow you transition to assisted living, memory care, and nursing without leaving the community or scrambling to find a bed. That continuity is the whole appeal — you (and a spouse) don't get separated or uprooted at the worst possible moment. The price is a structure with two parts: a substantial one-time entrance fee — commonly $150,000 to well over $1,000,000, often 50%, 75%, or 90% refundable to you or your estate — plus an ongoing monthly fee. What that money buys depends entirely on the contract type:

ContractHow care is pricedTrade-off
Type A — Life CareHigher levels of care included with little or no jump in the monthly fee.Highest entrance fee; you pre-pay to lock in future care cost.
Type B — ModifiedA set amount of higher-level care included, then market rates.Mid-range cost; shares risk between you and the community.
Type C — Fee-for-ServiceAccess to all levels, but you pay market rate for care as used.Lower entrance/monthly fee; you carry the future-care risk.
Type D — RentalNo entrance fee; full price for everything as you go.Lowest commitment; no pre-funding and no lock-in.

Because a CCRC is a decades-long relationship, diligence is part of the decision: read the contract type and refund terms, and check the community's financial health (occupancy, reserves, audited statements) — you are, in effect, prepaying an institution to be there for you in twenty years.

Paying for it — in brief. The four funding routes — self-fund, long-term-care insurance, a hybrid life/LTC policy, or Medicaid after spend-down — are worked through in Chapter 10. The one worth adding here, because it's really a housing decision in disguise, is family caregiving: the invisible default that quietly falls on a spouse or adult child, with real costs to their own health, career, and retirement. Where you choose to live directly shapes how heavily that burden lands — a CCRC or assisted living lifts it off the family in a way that aging in place alone does not.

If you relocate, lead with the right reasons. Many people move in retirement — nearer to family, to a warmer or cheaper place, or into a community. Weigh proximity to the people who'll show up for you and to good healthcare first; climate and cost of living second; and taxes (Chapter 21) last. A lower tax bill is a poor consolation if you're four states away from everyone you know when you need them. And whatever you choose, pair it with the documents in Chapter 30, so your care wishes are written down before anyone has to guess.

Ellen's family did the math in a hospital hallway; her neighbor did it a decade earlier and moved into a Type A CCRC at 72, so the same care now arrives at a predictable monthly fee with no move and no scramble. Same need, wildly different experience — and none of it is covered by Medicare. The families who do this well decide years ahead, with the numbers run.

Key action items

  • Settle the funding question in Chapter 10, then choose the setting. Decide self-fund vs. insurance vs. Medicaid there; here, decide where the care would happen — before a crisis decides for you.
  • Tour CCRCs about a decade before you'd move. Get the contract type and refund terms in writing, and review the community's financial statements — you're prepaying its future.
  • Make your current home care-ready. Single-floor living, a no-step entry, grab bars, and good lighting — cheap now, decisive later, and they let aging-in-place actually work.
  • If you'll relocate, rank people and healthcare above tax. Move toward the network that will show up for you; treat a lower tax bill as a tiebreaker, not the reason.
  • Write down your care preferences. Where you'd want to be, and who decides — captured in the directives of Chapter 30.
