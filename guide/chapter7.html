The 4% Rule, and How to Actually Execute It

Summary

  • The "4% rule" says you can withdraw 4% of your starting portfolio in year one, adjust that dollar amount for inflation each year after, and have a high chance of not running out over a 30-year retirement. It was always a rule of thumb, never a law.
  • It came from real research: Bill Bengen's 1994 study (which actually found 4.15%) and the 1998 Trinity Study, both using U.S. market history back to 1926.
  • Bengen himself has since raised his number. His 2025 book puts the "Universal SAFEMAX" at 4.7% for a diversified portfolio, and he suggests most current retirees can reasonably start at 5.25%–5.5%.
  • Forward-looking models disagree: Morningstar's late-2025 work lands near 3.9% because today's high stock valuations and modest bond yields imply lower future returns. Both camps are defensible — pick your number knowing why it differs.
  • The rule's blind spot is sequence risk: a bad market in your first few retirement years does far more damage than the same bad market later (see Chapter 20).
  • Executing it well means treating 4-ish% as a starting rate, staying flexible in bad years, and stress-testing your plan rather than trusting a single number.

Where the number came from. In October 1994, financial planner William Bengen published a study in the Journal of Financial Planning asking a simple question: what constant, inflation-adjusted withdrawal rate would have survived even the worst 30-year stretch in U.S. market history? Running every rolling 30-year period from 1926 on a 50/50 mix of large-cap stocks and intermediate Treasuries, he found the answer was about 4.15% — which the industry rounded to 4% and dubbed the "SAFEMAX," the maximum safe rate. The binding worst case was a retiree who started around 1968 and got hammered by a bear market followed by the high inflation of the 1970s. Four years later, three Trinity University professors (Cooley, Hubbard, and Walz) extended the analysis and found a 4% inflation-adjusted withdrawal had a 95%+ success rate over 30 years for stock-heavy portfolios. That's the "Trinity Study," and together the two cemented "4%" in the culture.

What "the rule" actually instructs. The mechanics matter and are widely misunderstood. You take 4% once, in year one. After that you ignore your balance and simply give the prior year's dollar withdrawal a raise equal to inflation. On a $1 million portfolio, that's $40,000 in year one; if inflation is 3%, you withdraw $41,200 the next year regardless of what the market did. The rule is a spending rule, not a percentage you recompute annually.

The number has moved — upward, from its own author. Bengen never intended 4% to be permanent. In his 2025 book, A Richer Retirement: Supercharging the 4% Rule to Spend More and Enjoy More, he rebuilt the study with a more diversified, seven-asset-class portfolio (55% stocks, 40% bonds, 5% cash) and a two-factor model that considers stock valuations (the Shiller CAPE ratio) and expected inflation. The new "Universal SAFEMAX" — the worst-case-proof rate — rose to 4.7%. On $1 million that's $47,000 instead of $40,000, a 17.5% raise. And Bengen goes further: because the truly catastrophic 1968-style setup is rare, the average safe rate across all his historical retirees was about 7.1%, and he argues most retirees today can reasonably start around 5.25%–5.5% even with elevated valuations.

...and downward, from the forecasters. Not everyone agrees, and the disagreement is instructive. Morningstar's late-2025 research put the 2026 safe starting rate near 3.9% for a moderate stock allocation with a 90% success target — lower than the classic 4%. Why the gap? Bengen's method is backward-looking (what survived history); Morningstar's is forward-looking (Monte Carlo simulations off today's capital-market assumptions, which bake in high U.S. equity valuations and lower expected bond returns than the 20th century delivered). Vanguard lands in between at roughly 3.5%–4.5%. Neither approach is "right." The historical camp says the future will rhyme with a very tough past; the forecasting camp says starting conditions matter and today's are expensive. Knowing which assumption you're leaning on is more useful than memorizing a single percentage.

The catch the rule hides: sequence risk. Two retirees can earn the exact same average return over 30 years and get wildly different outcomes if the order differs. Bad returns early, while you're also withdrawing, permanently shrink the base that later good years compound on. This is why a fixed 4% "rule" can still fail a 1968 retiree and why flexibility — trimming spending in down years — buys so much safety. Chapter 20 is devoted to it.

How to actually execute. Treat your withdrawal rate as a starting dial, not a vow. Pick a rate consistent with your horizon (longer retirements need lower rates — Bengen's own work shows a 60-year horizon drops the safe rate to about 4.1%), your allocation, and your tolerance for adjusting. Build in a plan to cut modestly if the first few years go badly, and to spend more if they go well. And remember Bengen's own verdict: inflation, not market crashes, is "the greatest enemy of retirees" — which is why the next chapter is about getting your inflation assumption right.

Key action items

  • Pick a starting rate on purpose. Choose based on your time horizon, stock/bond mix, and flexibility — not just "4% because everyone says so."
  • Decide your method for raises. The classic rule gives every year an inflation raise off the prior year's dollar amount. Know that's what you're signing up for.
  • Know which philosophy you're using. Backward-looking (Bengen, higher) vs. forward-looking (Morningstar, lower). Consider planning to the lower number and treating the higher as upside.
  • Lower the rate for long horizons. Early retirees (40+ year horizons) should think 3.25%–3.5%, not 4.7%.
  • Build in flexibility. Commit in advance to trimming spending after a bad early stretch; that single habit does more for success than fine-tuning the starting rate.
