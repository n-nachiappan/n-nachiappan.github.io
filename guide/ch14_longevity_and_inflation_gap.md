# Chapter 14 — The Long Tail: Longevity Risk and the Retiree Inflation Gap

### Summary of key points

- **Longevity risk** is the risk of living *longer* than your money — and it's the master risk that amplifies every other one in this book. The longer you live, the more inflation, healthcare, and sequence risk have time to bite.
- Planning to "average life expectancy" is a trap: an average means half of people live *longer*. For a 65-year-old couple, there's roughly a **50% chance at least one spouse reaches 90+**, and a meaningful chance of 95+.
- Plan to a **long horizon** — typically age 90–95, longer if your family history or health suggests it — not to the average.
- The **retiree inflation gap** (Chapter 2) compounds longevity risk: the longer you live, the more years that slightly-higher personal inflation rate erodes your purchasing power.
- Tools to hedge the long tail include **delaying Social Security** (Chapter 7, the best inflation-protected longevity hedge most people already own), **annuities/SPIAs** for mortality pooling, and **QLACs** — deferred annuities (max **$210,000** in 2026 under SECURE 2.0) that turn on late in life and are excluded from RMD calculations.
- This chapter ties the book together: build the plan to survive the *combination* of a long life, higher personal inflation, and a bad early market — not just the average case.

### Details

**Why longevity is the master risk.** Every other risk in this book gets worse the longer you live. A 0.3% inflation gap is trivial over 10 years and serious over 35. Healthcare's 5–6% climb is manageable for a while and overwhelming across three decades. A bad early market is survivable if you have a normal horizon and devastating if you also live to 98. Longevity doesn't just add years — it *multiplies* the impact of everything else. That's why it belongs last: it's the lens that makes the rest urgent.

**The averages trap.** People routinely plan to "life expectancy" — roughly 84 for a 65-year-old man, 87 for a woman. But life expectancy is a *median*: by definition, half of people outlive it. Planning your money to run out at the average is planning for a coin-flip chance of poverty in your final years. And couples face *joint* longevity: the relevant question isn't "how long will I live?" but "how long until *both* of us are gone?" For a healthy 65-year-old couple, there's roughly a **50% probability at least one spouse lives to 90 or beyond**, and a real chance of 95+. Bengen's own advice (Chapter 1) is to deliberately *overestimate* — if you think 25 years, plan for 30.

**The inflation gap over a long tail.** Chapter 2 showed retirees tend to experience modestly higher inflation than the headline index, driven by healthcare and housing. Over a long life that modest gap compounds into a real loss of purchasing power — the "silent" erosion that makes an 85-year-old's fixed income buy noticeably less than it did at 65. Combine longevity with the inflation gap and you get the core reason retirement plans should lean conservative: it's not one bad assumption, it's several plausible ones stacking across many years.

**Hedge 1 — delay Social Security (you may already own the best longevity insurance).** Social Security is the only widely held income that is *both* lifelong *and* inflation-adjusted *and* government-backed. Delaying the higher earner's benefit to 70 (Chapter 7) buys a larger, COLA-protected check for exactly the long-life scenario — and a larger survivor benefit for the spouse who lives longest. For most people, maximizing this is the single most cost-effective longevity hedge available, and it requires no insurance product at all.

**Hedge 2 — annuities and mortality pooling.** A single-premium immediate annuity (SPIA) converts a lump sum into guaranteed lifetime income. Its magic is **mortality pooling**: because some annuitants die early, the insurer can pay survivors more than a bond portfolio safely could — you're insured against the long-life risk you can't diversify away yourself. A common approach is to annuitize just *enough* to cover essential expenses (a "floor"), leaving the rest invested for growth and flexibility. Annuities aren't for everyone — they trade liquidity and legacy for security — but for covering baseline needs against a long life, they can be rational.

**Hedge 3 — QLACs for the far tail.** A **Qualified Longevity Annuity Contract** is a deferred annuity bought inside an IRA that starts paying later in life — you might buy it at 70 and have income begin at 80 or 85. Two features make it useful: the longer deferral means a much larger eventual payout, and under SECURE 2.0 the amount used to fund it (up to **$210,000 in 2026**, indexed) is **excluded from the balance your RMDs are calculated on** — so it both hedges longevity *and* trims your required distributions in your 70s. It's a targeted tool for people worried specifically about the expensive, uncertain final decade.

*Worked example.* A 70-year-old with a $1,000,000 IRA worries about running short if she lives to 95. She uses $200,000 to buy a QLAC that begins paying at 85. Two things happen: her RMDs from 73 onward are now calculated on $800,000 instead of $1,000,000 (lower forced income and taxes for over a decade), and at 85 a substantial guaranteed income kicks in for life — precisely when her invested portfolio is most at risk of depletion. She's converted "market risk in my 90s" into "known income in my 90s."

**Tying the book together.** The through-line of this guide is that retirement isn't one problem — it's a system of interacting risks. Longevity stretches the timeline; inflation (especially the retiree gap) erodes along it; healthcare spikes within it; sequence risk threatens its start; taxes, IRMAA, RMDs, and claiming decisions shape the cash flows all the way through. A good plan doesn't optimize any single piece — it's built to survive the *combination*: a long life, a higher-than-headline personal inflation rate, and a rough early market, all at once. If your plan holds under that trio, the average case takes care of itself.

### Key action items

- **Plan to a long horizon.** Use age 90–95 as a baseline; go longer given good health or family history. For couples, plan to the *second* death.
- **Stress the combination, not just the average.** Test long life *and* higher inflation *and* an early downturn together.
- **Maximize your built-in longevity insurance.** Delay the higher earner's Social Security to lock in the largest COLA-protected income and survivor benefit.
- **Consider a guaranteed income floor.** Evaluate a SPIA to cover essential expenses for life; annuitize needs, invest wants.
- **Look at a QLAC for the far tail.** If the final decade worries you, a deferred annuity both hedges it and lowers your RMDs.
- **Keep some growth for the long years.** A long horizon still needs equities (Chapter 13) to outpace a lifetime of inflation.

> **🧭 Model it.** Extend the calculator's horizon to 95 (or beyond) and re-run your plan — then layer on the stresses from earlier chapters: a higher inflation rate, a healthcare spike late in life, and a bad early market. If the plan survives that combination, it's robust. Where it breaks, you've found exactly which lever to pull — delay Social Security, trim spending, add a guaranteed-income floor — before it matters in real life rather than after.
