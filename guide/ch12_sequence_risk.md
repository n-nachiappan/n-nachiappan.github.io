# Chapter 12 — Sequence-of-Returns Risk and How to Blunt It

### Summary of key points

- **Sequence-of-returns risk** is the danger that *poor returns early in retirement* — while you're also withdrawing — do permanent damage, even if your long-run average return is fine.
- Two retirees with the *identical* average return over 30 years can end up rich or broke depending on the *order* of good and bad years. Order matters enormously once you're spending, not saving.
- The risk is concentrated in the **first 5–10 years** of retirement (the "fragile decade"). A crash then is far worse than the same crash later.
- Core mitigations: hold a **cash/bond buffer** so you don't sell stocks into a downturn; use **spending flexibility** (cut in bad years); consider **guardrails**; delay retirement or Social Security; and keep a sensible, diversified allocation.
- **Guyton-Klinger guardrails** allow higher starting withdrawals by adjusting spending up or down within bands — but critics (Kitces) note they can demand painful 30%+ cuts in a truly bad sequence. Newer **risk-based guardrails** aim to require much smaller adjustments.
- The cheapest, most reliable defense is **willingness to trim spending temporarily** when markets fall early.

### Details

**The mechanism.** In the accumulation phase, a market crash is almost a gift — you keep buying shares cheap and recover. In the *decumulation* phase, it's the opposite. When you sell shares to fund spending during a downturn, you lock in losses and sell *more* shares to raise the same dollars, permanently shrinking the base that later good years compound on. Even if the market eventually delivers a great average return, an early hole you sold into may never refill. That's sequence risk: same average, different order, different outcome.

*Worked illustration.* Imagine two retirees who each average 7% over their first decade while withdrawing. Retiree A gets a string of good years first, then a crash; Retiree B gets the crash in years one and two, then the good years. A's portfolio, having grown before the drawdown-plus-crash, absorbs the hit and recovers. B, having sold shares into the early crash, enters the recovery with far fewer shares and may run low decades later — despite the *identical* average return. The order alone separated them.

**The fragile decade.** Research consistently finds the danger is front-loaded: returns in roughly the **first 5–10 years** of retirement do the most to determine success or failure. A retiree who hits a bad market at 66 is in real jeopardy; the same market at 86 is a footnote. This is why the years right around your retirement date deserve the most caution — and why the classic 4% rule, which ignores order entirely, can still fail an unlucky 1968-style retiree.

**Mitigation 1 — a cash/bond buffer (the bucket approach).** Hold one to three years of spending in cash and short-term bonds. When stocks fall, spend from the buffer instead of selling equities into the decline, giving the stock portion time to recover. Refill the buffer in good years. This "bucket strategy" doesn't raise your long-run return, but it directly attacks the sequence-risk mechanism by removing the need to sell low.

**Mitigation 2 — spending flexibility.** The single most powerful and cheapest defense is a willingness to spend *less* temporarily when markets drop early. Even modest, temporary cuts in the first few bad years dramatically improve survival, because you preserve shares for the recovery. A retiree who can trim discretionary spending 10–15% in a downturn is vastly safer than one locked into a fixed real withdrawal.

**Mitigation 3 — guardrails (and their limits).** Guardrail systems formalize flexibility. The best-known, **Guyton-Klinger**, sets bands (commonly ±20%) around your withdrawal rate and cuts spending ~10% when you drift too high (or raises it when you're well below), which lets you *start* at a higher rate (5%+) because you'll adjust as you go. The catch, as Michael Kitces has pointed out, is that in a genuinely bad sequence real-world Guyton-Klinger can force *cumulative* cuts of 30% or more — a brutal lifestyle hit for someone already retired. Newer **risk-based guardrails** (Fitzpatrick/Tharp) trigger off your probability of success rather than fixed bands and tend to require far smaller adjustments — on the order of a few percent — for the same protection. The lesson: guardrails help, but know how deep the cuts can get in the worst case before you rely on a high starting rate.

**Mitigation 4 — delay or work a little longer.** Retiring into an expensive market is riskier; a short delay, part-time income, or delaying Social Security (Chapter 7) all reduce the portfolio's early burden. Even one or two years of continued earning shrinks the fragile decade's exposure.

**Mitigation 5 — sensible allocation.** Neither too-aggressive (deep early drawdowns) nor too-conservative (can't outrun inflation over 30 years). A diversified stock/bond mix with a modest cash reserve is the workhorse. Some advisors add a "rising equity glidepath" — starting retirement a bit more conservative and drifting *back* into stocks — precisely to reduce fragile-decade exposure.

### Key action items

- **Respect the fragile decade.** Be most cautious in the 5–10 years around your retirement date.
- **Hold a buffer.** Keep 1–3 years of spending in cash/short bonds so you never sell stocks into a crash.
- **Pre-commit to flexibility.** Decide *now* what you'll cut, and by how much, if the first few years go badly.
- **If you use guardrails, know the downside.** Understand how deep Guyton-Klinger cuts can go; consider risk-based guardrails for gentler adjustments.
- **Consider delaying.** A short work extension or delayed Social Security meaningfully lowers early risk.
- **Diversify and avoid extremes.** Keep an allocation that can survive an early crash *and* outrun 30 years of inflation.
