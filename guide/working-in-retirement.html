Working in Retirement

Summary

  • Roughly half of retirees do some paid work — phased retirement, consulting, a passion job, or simply not being ready to stop. It's mostly upside, but a paycheck ripples through four systems: Social Security, payroll tax, your retirement accounts, and Medicare.
  • The Social Security earnings test only bites before your full retirement age (FRA): $1 withheld for every $2 over $24,480 (2026); in the year you reach FRA, $1 per $3 over $65,160. From the month you hit FRA, you can earn any amount with no reduction.
  • Only earned income counts — wages and net self-employment. Pensions, annuities, investment income, and account withdrawals don't.
  • Withheld benefits aren't lost — at FRA your benefit is permanently recomputed upward to give them back.
  • Continued earnings can also raise your benefit for good: a new high year can replace a lower one in your top-35.
  • You still pay FICA on wages at any age — 7.65% as an employee, 15.3% self-employed — with no senior exemption.
  • Earned income lets you keep funding an IRA or Roth at any age (the old 70½ cap is gone), a workplace 401(k), and — until you enroll in Medicare — an HSA.
  • Past 65, if you (or a spouse) have coverage from an employer with 20+ employees, you can delay Medicare Part B penalty-free and enroll later through a Special Enrollment Period.
  • Enrolling in any part of Medicare — even premium-free Part A — ends HSA eligibility; mind the 6-month retroactive Part A trap.
  • Higher earnings push more of your Social Security into taxation and can trip IRMAA two years later.
  • The one combination that clearly costs you: claiming early and working before FRA. The cleanest pattern is to work and delay claiming.

A paycheck touches four systems. Continuing to earn in retirement is one of the most powerful — and least risky — moves you can make: every dollar earned is a dollar you don't withdraw, which blunts sequence risk and shortens the years your portfolio has to carry. But the folklore around it is mostly wrong. "You'll lose your Social Security" and "it's all taxed away anyway" scare people out of work that would help them. The reality is that a retirement paycheck runs through four separate systems — Social Security, payroll tax, your retirement accounts, and Medicare — each with one rule worth knowing. Walk them in order and the picture is far friendlier than the myths.

Social Security: the earnings test only bites early. If you've claimed and are under your full retirement age, the earnings test temporarily withholds benefits: in 2026, $1 for every $2 you earn above $24,480. In the calendar year you reach FRA the test softens to $1 for every $3 above $65,160, counting only what you earn in the months before your birthday. From the month you hit FRA onward, there is no earnings test at all — you can earn a million dollars and your check is untouched. Two things narrow the bite further. First, only earned income counts — wages and net self-employment. A pension, an annuity, a Roth conversion, an RMD, dividends, capital gains: none of it triggers the test. Second, the test doesn't shrink your monthly benefit — it suspends whole checks, then hands them back.

Withheld is not lost. This is the part almost everyone gets wrong. Money withheld by the earnings test isn't a penalty — it's a deferral. When you reach FRA, Social Security recomputes your benefit and permanently raises it to credit back the months it withheld — a higher monthly check for life, not a lump-sum refund — so over a normal life expectancy you're made roughly whole. The earnings test is best read not as "working destroys my benefit" but as "if I claimed early and I'm still earning real money, Social Security quietly nudges some of that benefit later — to a point when I'm past FRA and no longer working." That's a reason to coordinate: if you know you'll keep earning before FRA, it's usually cleaner to delay claiming in the first place (Chapter 13) than to claim early and have benefits withheld.

…and work can permanently raise your check. Separately from getting withheld benefits back, continued earnings can lift the benefit itself. Your Primary Insurance Amount is built from your 35 highest inflation-indexed earning years, and Social Security automatically recomputes it each year you have earnings. A solid part-time year late in life that displaces a $0 or low-earning year from early in your career nudges your benefit up — for life. It's a modest effect, but it's real, and it stacks on top of the withheld-benefit restoration above.

You still owe FICA — and it isn't wasted. There is no age exemption from payroll tax. As an employee you pay 7.65% — 6.2% for Social Security on wages up to the $184,500 wage base, plus 1.45% for Medicare with no cap — and your employer matches it. If you're self-employed you owe both halves, 15.3% (SECA), but you deduct half of it above the line. High earners add a 0.9% Medicare surtax on earnings over $200,000 single / $250,000 joint. Yes, you're paying into a system you're already drawing from — but it isn't pure cost: those very earnings feed the benefit recomputation above, so a slice of the FICA you pay can come back as a slightly larger check.

You can keep funding retirement accounts. Earned income is the key that unlocks contributions — and part-time wages or self-employment income qualify:

  • IRA and Roth IRA, at any age. The SECURE Act repealed the old rule that barred traditional-IRA contributions after 70½; Roth never had an age limit. In 2026 you can put in $7,500 plus a $1,100 catch-up at 50+, capped at your earned income if that's lower. Roth contributions still phase out at higher incomes, and a working spouse's income can fund a non-earning spouse's spousal IRA.
  • A workplace 401(k). If the part-time employer offers one and you're eligible — SECURE 2.0 now lets long-term part-timers in after two years of 500+ hours — you can defer up to $24,500 (plus $8,000 at 50+, or $11,250 at 60–63). Bonus: the "still-working" exception lets you postpone RMDs from that current employer's plan (if you own under 5% of the company) until you actually retire (Chapter 19).
  • An HSA — the most tax-efficient dollar there is. While you're covered by an HDHP and not yet enrolled in Medicare, you can still contribute ($4,400 self / $8,750 family, plus $1,000 at 55+). But Medicare enrollment shuts this door — see below — which is exactly why some people delay Medicare to keep the HSA open.
Worked example

The same $30,000 job — claimed early vs. after FRA

Susan's full retirement age is 67. She claimed early at 62, locking in a reduced $1,800/month ($21,600/year). At 63 she takes a part-time role paying $30,000. Watch what each system does — and what it doesn't.

1 Before FRA, the earnings test withholds a little — temporarily

What happens to the $30,000 jobAt 63 (before FRA)At 68 (after FRA)
Earnings over the $24,480 limit$5,520—
Benefits withheld ($1 per $2 over)$2,760$0
FICA payroll tax (7.65%)$2,295$2,295
Benefits permanently lost$0$0

To cover the $2,760, Social Security holds back whole monthly checks — here, about a month and a half's worth — and squares up any over-withholding. The only true out-of-pocket cost of the job is the FICA and the income tax on the earnings; the withheld benefit itself comes back. But how it comes back is the part almost everyone gets wrong.

2 How the withheld money returns — a bigger check, not a refund

Two separate things happen, and neither is a lump-sum reimbursement of the withheld dollars:

① The reduction is re-set at FRA. Claiming at 62 cut Susan's benefit ~30% for claiming 60 months early — that's how a ~$2,571 full benefit (her PIA) became the reduced $1,800 she collects. But months in which the earnings test withheld her whole check don't count as months she collected, so at 67 Social Security recomputes the reduction as if she had claimed those months later. Say she works all four years from 63 to 66 and about eight whole monthly checks get withheld along the way — roughly $11,040 in total, not one year's $2,760. The adjustment then treats her as if she first claimed at 62 years 8 months (52 months early instead of 60), easing the cut from 30% to about 26.7%:

How the FRA step-up is figuredEarly-claiming cutMonthly check
Claimed at 62y 0m — as originally filed−30.0%$1,800
Re-set at 67 to 62y 8m — ≈8 months withheld−26.7%$1,886

That's about $86 more a month, for life (before COLAs) — the withheld ~$11,040 handed back as a permanently higher check, not a lump sum. At roughly $1,030 a year, it takes into her late 70s to draw level, which is exactly why the adjustment is "break-even over an average lifespan" rather than a windfall.

② The PIA itself may rise — going forward only. That $30,000 is also a fresh year of earnings. If it beats one of the 35 years behind her PIA, Social Security automatically recomputes the PIA upward. The catch: the higher PIA takes effect prospectively — from about the January after the earnings year — not retroactively to her age-62 claim. Her earlier reduced checks stand as paid; there is no one-time back-payment to "true up" the years she collected on the lower PIA. She simply draws a slightly larger benefit from the recomputation date onward (and if that date is still before FRA, the increase is itself reduced for early claiming and then picked up in the same FRA adjustment above).

3 After FRA, the bite disappears entirely

Had Susan taken the exact same job at 68, the earnings test wouldn't touch a dollar of her benefit — she'd keep the full $21,600 and the $30,000, with no reduction to unwind later. That's the core lesson: working rarely costs you Social Security, and past FRA it never does. If you'll be earning meaningful money in your early-to-mid 60s, the cleanest play is usually to work and delay claiming — you sidestep the earnings test and the whole restore-it-later machinery, and lock in a larger, survivor-protected benefit at the same time.

Medicare when you keep working past 65. Turning 65 while still employed doesn't force you onto Medicare — but the rules hinge on how big the employer is, and getting them wrong is expensive.

  • Employer with 20+ employees (your active coverage or a working spouse's): the group plan stays primary and Medicare is secondary. You can delay Part B and Part D penalty-free and enroll later through an 8-month Special Enrollment Period after the job or that coverage ends. No lifelong late penalty.
  • Employer with fewer than 20 employees: Medicare is generally primary at 65. Enroll in Part A and Part B on time or you risk coverage gaps and a permanent Part B late penalty of 10% for each 12 months you delay. Confirm how your specific plan coordinates before you skip anything.
  • The HSA collision. Enrolling in any part of Medicare — including premium-free Part A — ends your HSA eligibility going forward. To keep contributing past 65 you must delay all of Medicare, which in practice means not yet claiming Social Security, since claiming at 65+ auto-enrolls you in Part A. And mind the trap: when you finally enroll after 65, Part A backdates up to six months, so stop HSA contributions six months before you enroll or file for Social Security, or you'll owe a 6% excise tax on the excess.
  • The income echo. A strong work year can push your income into an IRMAA tier — but on a two-year lookback, so earnings at 65 surface as a Medicare surcharge at 67. Higher income can also make more of your Social Security taxable. Neither is a reason not to work; both are reasons to see the full bill.

If you're retiring before 65 and working part-time, the same earnings feed one more calculation: your ACA premium tax credit is income-based, so part-time wages can shrink the subsidy that makes pre-Medicare coverage affordable (Chapter 23). Between 65 and FRA the tension flips — Medicare has replaced the ACA, so the only thing left to manage is the earnings test and your tax bracket.

Key action items

  • Know whether the earnings test applies to you this year. It only matters if you've claimed and you're under FRA. If you'll earn well over the limit, consider delaying your claim instead of having benefits withheld.
  • Don't treat withholding as a loss. Benefits held back by the earnings test are restored at FRA through a permanently higher check.
  • Keep funding tax-advantaged accounts. Earned income unlocks an IRA or Roth at any age, your workplace plan, and — until Medicare — the HSA. Fund them.
  • If working past 65, check the employer's size. 20+ employees means you can safely delay Part B; under 20 usually means enroll on time.
  • To keep your HSA past 65, delay all of Medicare — including Part A — and stop contributions six months before you eventually enroll or claim Social Security.
  • Look two years ahead. Watch the IRMAA lookback and the extra Social Security taxation that a bigger paycheck can trigger.
