Reference · Appendix G

Asset Location: What Goes in Which Account

The idea

  • Appendix D asks how each account is taxed. This one asks the next question: given several accounts, which investment goes in which one? That's asset location — and it's free money, in the narrow sense that it changes your after-tax result without changing your risk.
  • The rule in one line: put the assets the tax code punishes hardest inside the wrappers that shield them, and leave the assets the code already treats gently in the taxable account. Bonds, REITs, and high-turnover funds inside; broad stock index funds and low-dividend growth outside.
  • Don't expect miracles. Credible estimates of the benefit cluster around 10–50 basis points a year — real, worth capturing, but an order of magnitude smaller than getting your savings rate (F1) or your stock/bond split (F3) right. Location is a refinement, never a substitute.
  • Location must not break diversification. The classic failure is stuffing every bond into the 401(k) and every stock into the Roth, then discovering you can no longer rebalance and your true after-tax allocation is more aggressive than you thought. §6 covers the fix.
  • Two popular rules of thumb are only half right: "growth assets belong in the Roth" (§4) and "REITs always belong in the IRA" (§2). Both survive with caveats, and it's worth knowing which.
  • Inheritance flips one of the answers. The step-up in basis makes a taxable brokerage account an excellent place to die holding appreciated stock — so if you're leaving money behind, don't be too eager to shelter it (§5, and Ch 27).

Related: Ch 6 (taxable accounts and Treasuries) · Ch 11 (brackets) · Ch 16 (the IOVA as overflow shelter) · Ch 19 (the mirror-image problem: which bucket to spend)

1 · Two decisions, not one

Allocation decides your risk; location decides your tax bill. Asset allocation is how much you hold in stocks versus bonds — the decision that drives essentially all of your return and all of your volatility. Asset location is which account each of those holdings physically sits in. Allocation should be settled first and never bent to serve location. Once it's settled, location is a second, independent lever that costs you nothing in risk.

Why it works at all. The tax code doesn't tax "investment returns" — it taxes kinds of return at wildly different rates. Bond interest, REIT distributions, and short-term gains are ordinary income, taxed up to 37% (plus the 3.8% NIIT). Qualified dividends and long-term gains get 0/15/20%, and unrealized appreciation gets taxed at nothing at all until you sell. An account wrapper that erases annual taxation is therefore worth far more wrapped around a bond fund than around a buy-and-hold index fund that was barely being taxed anyway.

How big is the prize? Honest numbers: Daryanani & Cordaro (2005) put it near 20 bps/yr; Blanchett & Kaplan's "Gamma" paper (2013) attributes roughly 23 bps to location alone (about 52 bps for retirees) out of 182 bps of total planning value; Vanguard's work lands around 30 bps for broad asset-class location, plus up to another 10 bps for sorting equity sub-classes. Anything advertised above 100 bps is quietly bundling in withdrawal sequencing or Roth conversions, which are different chapters (19 and 13).

The REIT asterisk. Qualified REIT dividends get the 20% §199A deduction, and the deduction is not limited by the W-2-wage or property tests that constrain business owners — a passive REIT holder gets the full 20%. The One Big Beautiful Bill Act (July 2025) repealed the 2025 sunset and made §199A permanent, so it is live for 2026 (older articles saying it expires are stale). That drops a top-bracket investor's rate on REIT dividends from 37% to 29.6% — and that break exists only in a taxable account, since money inside a traditional IRA comes out at the full ordinary rate regardless. REITs still usually belong in the shelter on deferral grounds, but the margin is narrower than the old rule of thumb suggests.

4 · "Put the growth in the Roth" — half right

The popular argument. A Roth is never taxed again, so every dollar of growth inside it is yours. Put your highest-expected-return assets there and you maximize the amount compounding tax-free. A dollar growing at 8% inside a tax-free wrapper is worth more than a dollar growing at 4%.

The rebuttal, and it's a good one. Reichenstein & Meyer's insight is that a pre-tax account is a partnership with the government: if you'll withdraw at a 24% rate, a $100,000 traditional IRA is really $76,000 of your money and $24,000 of the government's. A $100,000 Roth is $100,000 of yours. Comparing raw balances across account types is an apples-to-oranges error. And because the government's share is a fixed percentage, it shrinks when the account falls — the government shares your downside too. So when you move equities into the Roth and bonds into the traditional account, you haven't conjured return out of the tax code; you've quietly raised your true after-tax equity exposure. The extra expected return is payment for extra risk, which you could have taken more honestly by just raising your stock allocation.

Where it nets out. If your tax rate is the same going in and coming out, traditional and Roth produce identical after-tax wealth no matter what's inside them — the Roth's edge comes from rate arbitrage (contributing or converting cheap, withdrawing dear), not from its contents. That said, two genuinely tax-driven reasons to favor equities in a Roth survive the critique intact: a Roth IRA has no RMDs, so the tax-free compounding runs longer than a traditional account's does; and it passes to heirs tax-free while a traditional account passes as ordinary income (§5). Both argue for the long-horizon, high-growth asset. Just don't tell yourself it's free.

A practical caveat on the rebuttal itself: using your marginal rate overstates the government's share, because most retirees withdraw across several brackets and their effective rate is lower. With realistic numbers, tax-adjusting your allocation usually shifts it only modestly. Treat this as a correction to your reasoning, not usually a large change to your portfolio.

5 · The inheritance overlay

Everything above optimizes for the money you'll spend. If some of it will instead be inherited, one rule dominates all the others: the step-up in basis (Ch 27). Assets in a taxable brokerage account have their cost basis reset to date-of-death value, permanently erasing a lifetime of unrealized gains. Nothing else in the code is that generous.

WrapperStep-up?What the heir owesTimetable
Taxable brokerageYesNothing on pre-death appreciationNone — hold forever
Roth IRAN/ATax-freeEmpty in 10 years; no annual RMDs
Traditional IRA / 401(k)No — it's IRDOrdinary income at the heir's rateEmpty in 10 years
Non-qualified annuity / IOVANo — also IRDOrdinary income on the whole gain5-year rule, or a life-expectancy stretch if offered

What that changes. Three things. First, low-basis, high-growth stock should stay in the taxable account if you expect to leave it behind — the embedded gain is forgiven entirely. Moving that same asset into a traditional IRA converts a 0% eventual tax into ordinary income for your heirs, which is the most expensive location mistake available. Second, the standard shape — bonds in the IRA, appreciated equity in taxable — happens to be exactly right for a bequest plan, which is a rare piece of luck. Third, the IOVA is the worst wrapper to die holding: no step-up, ordinary income, and a compressed five-year window for the heir. Use it for assets you intend to spend, not to bequeath.

A quick sense of scale: $300,000 of stock bought for $100,000 carries a $200,000 embedded gain. Sell it in retirement at 15% and you owe $30,000; die holding it and your heirs owe $0. That $30,000 is the step-up's value on a single position — and it's available only in the taxable account.

6 · Where the rules break

Don't let location wreck your diversification. This is the most common real-world failure, and it has three faces. (1) Your allocation isn't what you think — the 401(k) balance overstates your share of it by your marginal rate, so a nominal 60/40 held with all bonds in the 401(k) may be closer to 65/35 after tax. (2) Rebalancing breaks. If the Roth and taxable accounts hold nothing but equities, there's no bond position there to rebalance into, so you must either sell appreciated stock in taxable — realizing the gains you were trying to defer — or let the portfolio drift. (3) Location drifts on its own, because equities compound faster, so the equity-only accounts grow as a share of the whole. The fix: keep a partial bond position in each account so rebalancing is always possible somewhere, and rebalance with new contributions and withdrawals rather than sales. The zero-complexity baseline — the same allocation in every account — is a perfectly respectable answer, and it's the benchmark any location scheme has to beat.

The foreign tax credit argues for international equity in taxable. Foreign countries withhold tax at source on dividends paid to US investors — typically 7–8% of the dividend for a broad developed-markets index fund. In a taxable account you recover it via the foreign tax credit. In an IRA or 401(k) there's no current US tax to credit it against, so the withholding is a permanent leak. Worth roughly 10–25 bps — a tiebreaker, not a driver. Caveats: the credit is capped by your US tax on foreign income, and a fund only passes it through if more than half its assets are foreign, so a total-world fund may not qualify.

Never put munis or tax-managed funds in an IRA. You'd be paying for tax efficiency you can't use. A muni yields less precisely because its interest is exempt — put it in a traditional IRA and you accept the low yield and convert the exempt interest into fully taxable ordinary income on withdrawal. Same logic for tax-managed funds, which charge more and accept tracking error to buy benefits worth exactly zero inside a shelter. The muni breakeven is simple:

The muni breakeven

Taxable-equivalent yield = muni yield ÷ (1 − your marginal rate)
Breakeven marginal rate = 1 − (muni yield ÷ taxable yield)

A 4.00% muni against a 4.75% corporate breaks even at 1 − (4.00 ÷ 4.75) = 15.8% — so any investor above a 15.8% marginal rate prefers the muni. At 24%, that muni's taxable-equivalent yield is 4.00 ÷ 0.76 = 5.26%. Include the 3.8% NIIT and your state rate where they apply, since muni interest escapes both.

MLPs inside an IRA can generate a tax bill with no withdrawal. An IRA holding a master limited partnership becomes a partner in an operating business, and that income is unrelated business taxable income (UBTI). Past $1,000 of total positive UBTI across the whole account, the IRA itself must file Form 990-T and pay tax out of IRA assets — at compressed trust rates that reach 37% around $15,650. A tax-advantaged asset in a tax-advantaged account, manufacturing a current tax bill. Hold MLPs in taxable.

Tax-loss harvesting only exists in taxable — and there's a trap. Losses inside an IRA are never recognized, which is a quiet argument for holding volatile assets in taxable, at least in the early years. But under Rev. Rul. 2008-5, if you sell at a loss in your taxable account and buy a substantially identical security in your IRA or Roth IRA within the 61-day window, the loss is disallowed and — unlike an ordinary wash sale — it is permanently forfeited, because your IRA basis doesn't get the offsetting increase. Brokers don't track this across institutions, so nothing on your 1099-B will warn you.

Bonds in a Roth are usually the wrong call — not because of the risk-shifting argument in §4, but because the Roth is your scarcest wrapper and a bond is a low-return asset. If bonds must live in a shelter, the traditional account is their home. The ordering for fixed income is: traditional → Roth → taxable. One honest caveat: when yields are very low, the tax drag on a 2% coupon is trivial and bonds fall into a "zone of indifference" where prioritizing them into shelters barely matters. This ranking assumes today's higher-yield environment.

Worked example

The same portfolio, located two ways

Meera and Sam, both 55 and in the 32% bracket, hold $1,200,000 at a 60/40 allocation — $720,000 in a total-stock index fund yielding 2% in qualified dividends, and $480,000 in a taxable bond fund yielding 4.5%. It sits across three accounts: a $600,000 traditional 401(k), a $200,000 Roth IRA, and a $400,000 taxable brokerage. The allocation is identical in both scenarios below — only the placement changes.

1 Mirrored — each account held 60/40, the zero-thought default

The taxable account holds $240,000 of stock and $160,000 of bonds. Only the taxable account generates a yearly tax bill:

bonds: $160,000 × 4.5% × 32% = $2,304  +  dividends: $240,000 × 2% × 15% = $720 → $3,024/yr

2 Located — the entire bond sleeve moved into the 401(k)

All $480,000 of bonds fits inside the $600,000 401(k), which holds the remaining $120,000 in stock. The Roth and the taxable account are now 100% stock:

bonds: $0 taxed annually  +  dividends: $400,000 × 2% × 15% = $1,200 → $1,200/yr

3 Compound the difference over the 20 years to age 75

$1,824/yr saved, reinvested at 6% → $1,824 × 36.79 = ~$67,000

The annual saving is $1,824 — about 15 basis points on a $1.2M portfolio, squarely inside the 10–50 bps range the research supports, and worth roughly $67,000 by 75. Real money for an afternoon's work, and no extra risk taken. But note the two strings attached. Their taxable and Roth accounts now hold no bonds, so all rebalancing has to happen inside the 401(k) — which is fine, because it's big enough to hold the whole bond sleeve with room to spare. Had it not been, they'd need to leave a bond position in a second account to rebalance against. And their $400,000 of taxable stock is now the account carrying all the embedded gains — exactly where they want it if it's headed to their children, since the step-up will erase them (§5).

Rules and dollar figures are for the 2026 plan year. Long-term capital-gain and ordinary brackets are per Rev. Proc. 2025-32 (see Appendix C); the 3.8% NIIT thresholds of $200,000 single / $250,000 joint are fixed in statute and not indexed, so they reach further down the income scale each year. Tax-cost ratios and fee figures are category averages that vary widely by fund and by year. Asset location is sensitive to your bracket now versus later, your state, your horizon, and whether you intend to spend or bequeath — the rankings here are defaults, not rules. Nothing here is personalized financial, tax, or legal advice; confirm your own situation with a CPA or fiduciary advisor.
