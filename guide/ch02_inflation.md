# Chapter 2 — Inflation: Why Your Number Is Probably Wrong

### Summary of key points

- The single assumption that quietly makes or breaks a 30-year plan is not your return — it's your **inflation rate**. Small errors compound into large ones.
- Most planners default to a flat 2–2.5%. Retirees don't spend like the "all urban consumer" the official index is built around. Your personal inflation rate depends on **what you buy**, and retirees buy more healthcare and housing and less of everything else.
- The government publishes an experimental index for older Americans — the **R-CPI-E** — that has historically run modestly higher than the headline CPI-U, but the gap is small, varies by period, and has even reversed. Don't overclaim it; do account for it.
- Ironically, your Social Security raise (the COLA) is tied to **CPI-W**, an index built from the spending of *working* wage earners — arguably the population least like you.
- The practical move isn't to find the "true" retiree inflation number. It's to **personalize your assumption to your spending mix** and then **stress-test a higher one**, because the cost of underestimating is far worse than the cost of overestimating.

### Details

**Why inflation is the assumption that matters most.** Over a single year, a half-point of inflation is a rounding error. Over a retirement, it's a different retirement. Suppose you need $80,000 a year in today's dollars. Grow that at 2.5% for 25 years and the bill in year 25 is about $148,000. Grow it at 3.0% instead — just half a point higher — and it's about $168,000. That half-point error means your final-year spending is roughly **13% higher** than you planned, and every year in between was short too. The gap doesn't add; it compounds. This is why an honest inflation assumption beats a precise return forecast: markets recover, but a structurally low inflation assumption bleeds you for three decades.

**Why "the" inflation rate isn't your inflation rate.** The headline number you hear on the news is the **CPI-U**, the Consumer Price Index for All Urban Consumers. It reflects the spending basket of about 90% of the U.S. population — students, families, workers, and retirees averaged together. But a basket is only representative if it looks like your cart. Retirees systematically over-weight two categories that tend to inflate faster than average: **medical care** and **housing**. They under-weight categories like transportation, apparel, and education. When your basket tilts toward the fast-inflating items, your personal inflation rate drifts above the average — sometimes a little, sometimes a lot, depending on the decade and your own health spending.

**What the data actually shows (and the honest caveats).** Congress recognized this in the Older Americans Act of 1987 and directed the Bureau of Labor Statistics to build an index for Americans 62 and older. That index — now published as the **R-CPI-E**, and explicitly labeled *research/experimental* — has over the long run risen slightly faster than the official measures: from late 1982 through 2011, roughly 3.1% per year versus about 2.9% for CPI-U. That's a gap of about **0.2 percentage points a year**, driven mainly by the larger weight of medical care in older households. But be careful not to overstate it. The gap isn't constant, and it has reversed: from 2006 to 2011, the elderly index and CPI-U both rose about 2.3% a year. The R-CPI-E also carries real methodological limits — it reuses the same stores, geographic areas, and item samples chosen for the general urban population, so it doesn't fully capture where and how older people actually shop. The takeaway is not "add 0.2% and you're done." It's "your basket is different, that difference usually cuts against you, and the size is uncertain — so plan defensively."

**The COLA irony.** You might assume Social Security protects you from all this. It helps, but the annual cost-of-living adjustment is pegged to **CPI-W** — the index for urban *wage earners and clerical workers*, a fully employed population by definition. That's arguably the group least like a retiree. When medical inflation runs hot, a CPI-W-based COLA can systematically lag what retirees actually experience. This is also why your COLA can feel like it "doesn't keep up" even when the raise is technically inflation-adjusted — it's adjusted for someone else's inflation.

**So what number should you use?** There's no single right answer, but there is a right *method*: start from the headline long-run average (call it ~2.5–3%), then nudge it up based on how healthcare- and housing-heavy your spending is, and then run the plan again at a rate half a point to a point higher as a stress test. If your plan survives the higher number, you've bought resilience. If it only survives the low number, you've built a plan that depends on the most optimistic assumption in the model.

### Key action items

- **Build your basket.** List your annual spending by category. If healthcare + housing is a large share, assume your personal inflation runs above the headline rate.
- **Pick a base assumption, then stress it.** Choose a reasonable base (2.5–3%), then re-run at +0.5 to +1 point. Treat the higher run as the real test.
- **Model healthcare separately if you can.** Because medical inflation historically outruns general inflation, break it out rather than burying it in one blended rate (see Chapter 3).
- **Don't count on the COLA to fully protect you.** Treat Social Security's raise as partial inflation protection, not complete.
- **Revisit annually.** Inflation regimes shift; re-check against your own spending, not just the headline print.

> **🧭 Model it.** In the calculator, change the inflation assumption and watch how the *terminal* years move — that's where a small rate change does its damage. Run the plan at your base rate, then bump inflation up half a point and note how much your required withdrawals and end-of-plan shortfall shift. If a half-point swing breaks the plan, that's your signal to build in more cushion — not to hope for the low number.
