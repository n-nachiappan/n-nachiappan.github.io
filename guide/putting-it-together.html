Capstone · Bringing It Together

Putting It All Together

The idea

  • Twenty-seven chapters come down to a short list of durable ideas and a shorter list of things to actually do. This closing chapter is both — the core guide you can hold in your head at once.
  • Retirement is three problems, not one: build the savings, spend it down without running out, and pass on what's left. First the key takeaways (the why), then the action plan (the what to do).
  • Every move here links back to the chapter that develops it — and run any decision through the calculator against your own numbers before you act.

Part 1 · The key takeaways

Retirement is three problems, not one — and each has a single organizing idea.

ProblemPhaseThe one ideaPart
BuildAccumulationSave in the right accounts, in the right order — and let tax-advantaged compounding do the work.I
SpendDecumulationTurn the pile into a paycheck that lasts — net of taxes, Medicare, inflation, and a long life.II–III
Pass onLegacyLeave what's left the tax-smart way — the step-up and Roth to heirs, the pre-tax to yourself.IV

Building the pot — Part I

  • The match is free money. Capturing the full employer match is the highest-return move in personal finance — often an instant 100% return. It comes before every other dollar. (Ch 1)
  • There's a savings waterfall. Match first → fill tax-advantaged space (401(k), IRA/Roth, HSA) → then taxable. Order matters as much as amount. (Ch 1–6)
  • Pre-tax vs. Roth is one bet: your tax rate now vs. later. Pre-tax if lower later, Roth if higher. When unsure, hold all three buckets — pre-tax, Roth, taxable — for the tax diversification you'll exploit in retirement. (Ch 1, 3)
  • The HSA is the only triple-tax-advantaged account. Deductible in, growth tax-free, tax-free out for medical — invest it, don't spend it, and run it as a stealth retirement fund. (Ch 4)
  • Social Security is your inflation-linked floor. A progressive, COLA-protected, guaranteed base earned over your highest 35 years — the foundation the rest of the plan sits on. (Ch 5)

Spending it down — Part II

  • 4% is a starting line, not a law. A reasonable first-year rate (Bengen's 4%, updated to ~4.7%; lower for 40-year horizons), not a guarantee. Flexibility in bad early years matters more than the exact rate. (Ch 7)
  • Your inflation number is probably too low — and healthcare is worse. Retirees spend differently than the CPI basket; medical costs inflate ~5–6% a year and run ~$172,500 per person. Budget healthcare as its own line. (Ch 9, 10)
  • In retirement you finally control your tax bill. You choose how much income to realize each year. Smooth the rate, fill low brackets, mind the Social Security "tax torpedo," and harvest the 0% gains bracket. (Ch 11)
  • The gap years are the hinge. The window between leaving work and starting Social Security / RMDs is your lowest-tax era — the time to Roth-convert and fill cheap brackets before RMDs force your hand. (Ch 13, 19)
  • IRMAA is a cliff, on a two-year lookback. One dollar over a threshold bumps your whole Medicare tier. Convert to the line, not over it. (Ch 12)
  • Delaying Social Security is longevity insurance. ~8%/yr of delay to 70 — and the higher earner's timing sets the survivor benefit for both lives. Frame it as insurance, not a breakeven bet. (Ch 14)
  • You can buy guaranteed income. Pensions, plain SPIAs/QLACs, and a home-equity line cover essentials beyond Social Security — annuitize the needs, invest the wants. (Ch 15–17)
  • Sequencing is the hub. Which bucket you tap ties together Roth conversions, Social Security timing, IRMAA, and RMDs — one coordinated income strategy, not five separate ones. (Ch 19, 20)
  • Sequence risk and longevity are the master threats. A bad return sequence in the "fragile decade" does permanent damage; a long life multiplies every other risk. Defend with a cash buffer, spending flexibility, and delayed Social Security. (Ch 8, 22)
  • Time in the market is the engine. A century of returns rewards patience, low costs, and diversification — keep growth for the long tail, and don't sell into a downturn. (Ch F4)

Retiring early — Part III

  • Two doors open your money before 59½. The Rule of 55 (penalty-free from your current 401(k) if you leave at 55+ — don't roll it to an IRA first) and 72(t)/SEPP (a rigid schedule at any age you must not bust). (Ch 23, 24)
  • Health coverage is the other bridge. From an early exit to 65, COBRA / a spouse's plan / the ACA marketplace fill the gap — and MAGI is the lever that sets your premium tax credit. (Ch 25)

Passing it on — Part IV

  • It's the step-up, not the estate tax. With a $15M/person federal exemption, estate tax is irrelevant for nearly every family. The real game is income-tax character. (Ch 26)
  • Leave the right assets to the right place. Taxable brokerage (basis step-up) and Roth (tax-free) to heirs; spend or convert the pre-tax yourself — heirs owe ordinary income on it and must empty it within 10 years. (Ch 26)

That's the whole guide in one breath: build in the right accounts, spend in the right order, and pass on the right assets. Everything else is detail — and the detail is in the chapters. Now, the concrete moves.

Part 2 · The action plan

Not every action item from all 27 chapters — the ones with the most leverage, organized by part, in roughly the order life hands them to you. Do the ones that apply to you.

PART I · ACCUMULATION

Build the pot

  • Capture the full employer match — always. It's free money, an instant ~100% return; never leave any on the table. (Ch 1)
  • Max tax-advantaged space, in order. 401(k) $24,500 (+$8,000 at 50+, +$11,250 at 60–63), then IRA $7,500 (+$1,100 at 50+). (Ch 1, 2)
  • Use the backdoor Roth if you're over the income limits. Fund a non-deductible IRA, then convert — but clear out pre-tax IRA money first to dodge the pro-rata rule. (Ch 3)
  • Max the HSA — the only triple tax break. $4,400 self / $8,750 family (+$1,000 at 55+). Invest it; pay medical bills out of pocket and save receipts. (Ch 4)
  • Check your Social Security record. Verify earnings and credits, and aim for 35 real earning years to avoid $0s dragging the average. (Ch 5)
  • Build all three tax buckets. Pre-tax, Roth, and taxable — the tax diversification you'll exploit in retirement. (Ch 3, 6)

PART II · DECUMULATION

The spend-down

  • Pick a withdrawal rate on purpose. Start near 4–4.7%; use 3.25–3.5% for 40-year horizons. Commit in advance to trimming in bad early years. (Ch 7)
  • Budget healthcare as its own line. ~$172,500 per person over retirement — inflate it at 5–6%, not 3%, and choose your Medicare structure before your enrollment window. (Ch 10)
  • Work the gap years. Before Social Security and RMDs, deliberately fill low brackets — Roth-convert and harvest 0% gains while your rate is lowest. (Ch 11, 13)
  • Mind the IRMAA cliffs. Tier 1 hits at $109k single / $218k joint on a two-year lookback. Convert to the line, not over it. (Ch 12)
  • Delay the higher earner's Social Security to 70. ~+8%/yr of delay — and it sets the survivor benefit for both lives. (Ch 14)
  • Sequence to fill brackets; shrink pre-tax before RMDs. RMDs begin at 73→75; once 70½, give with QCDs (up to $108,000) to satisfy them tax-free. (Ch 19, 20)
  • Cover essentials with guaranteed income if it fits. A pension election, a plain SPIA, or a QLAC for the far tail — annuitize the gap above Social Security, not the whole nest egg. (Ch 15, 16)
  • Keep earning if you want to — it's mostly upside. The Social Security earnings test only bites before FRA and hands the money back later; you can still fund an IRA, Roth, or HSA; and past 65 an employer with 20+ workers lets you delay Medicare Part B. Work and delay claiming. (Ch 18)

PART III · ACCESS

Early access — before 59½

  • Prefer the Rule of 55 if you qualify. Penalty-free from your current 401(k) if you leave at 55+ — don't roll it to an IRA first. (Ch 24)
  • Use 72(t) / SEPP for IRA access at any age. Split the IRA first, size the payment carefully, and never bust the schedule (5 years or 59½). (Ch 23)
  • Bridge health coverage to 65. COBRA, a spouse's plan, or ACA — manage MAGI to capture income-based premium tax credits, and watch the 400% FPL cliff. (Ch 25)
  • Size early withdrawals to your bracket. Every dollar is ordinary income; don't spike your rate just to reach money early. (Ch 11, 19)

Survive the long run

  • Defend the fragile decade. The first 5–10 years drive success — a bad early sequence does permanent damage. (Ch 8)
  • Hold a 1–3 year cash buffer. So you never have to sell stocks into a crash to fund spending. (Ch 8)
  • Pre-commit to flexibility. Decide now what you'll cut, and by how much, after a bad start. (Ch 8)
  • Plan to 90–95+. For couples, plan to the second death — longevity multiplies every other risk. (Ch 22)
  • Keep growth for the long tail. Stay diversified in equities and cut costs — a century of data rewards patience. (Ch F4)

PART IV · LEGACY

The tax-smart handoff

  • Leave the step-up assets to heirs. Taxable brokerage gets a basis step-up at death — a lifetime of gains erased. (Ch 26)
  • Leave Roth to heirs too. It passes income-tax-free, with 10 more years of tax-free growth. (Ch 26)
  • Spend or convert the pre-tax yourself. Heirs owe ordinary income on IRA/401(k) and must empty it within 10 years. (Ch 26)
  • Give from the IRA with QCDs. After 70½, route gifts directly — tax-free, and it counts toward your RMD. (Ch 20, 26)
  • Review beneficiary designations now. They override your will — update after every marriage, divorce, birth, or death. Don't over-plan for estate tax ($15M/person). (Ch 26)
