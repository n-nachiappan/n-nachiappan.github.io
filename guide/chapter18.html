RMDs and SECURE 2.0

Summary

  • Required Minimum Distributions (RMDs) are the IRS's way of forcing you to eventually withdraw — and pay tax on — the money you deferred in traditional 401(k)s and IRAs.
  • Under SECURE 2.0, RMDs now begin at age 73 (for those born 1951–1959) and will rise to 75 for those born in 1960 or later, starting in 2033.
  • The amount is your prior-year-end balance divided by a life-expectancy factor from the IRS Uniform Lifetime Table; the required percentage grows every year as you age.
  • Miss one and the penalty is now 25% of the shortfall (reduced from 50%), dropping to 10% if you correct it promptly.
  • Roth IRAs have no RMDs for the original owner, and as of 2024 Roth 401(k)s are exempt too — a key reason to convert or use Roth accounts (Chapter 12).
  • Two powerful softeners: Qualified Charitable Distributions (QCDs) let you send RMD money to charity tax-free, and planning in your 60s (conversions, drawdowns) shrinks the balance RMDs are based on.

What RMDs are and why they exist. Traditional retirement accounts are a deal with the government: you skip tax on contributions and growth for decades, but the IRS eventually wants its cut. RMDs enforce that — starting at your RMD age, you must withdraw a minimum amount each year and pay ordinary income tax on it, whether or not you need the money. The rules apply to traditional IRAs, 401(k)s, 403(b)s, and similar pre-tax accounts.

The ages under SECURE 2.0. The starting age has been marching upward. It was 70½ for years, moved to 72, and under the SECURE 2.0 Act of 2022 became 73 for anyone reaching that age after 2022 (born 1951–1959). It's scheduled to rise again to 75 for those born in 1960 or later, effective 2033. Knowing your exact RMD age matters, because it defines the length of your low-tax "gap years" for conversions and bracket-filling.

How the amount is computed. Each year's RMD equals your account balance as of the prior December 31, divided by a factor from the IRS Uniform Lifetime Table. At 73 the factor is about 26.5 (roughly a 3.8% withdrawal); the factor shrinks each year, so the percentage you must take climbs steadily — around 4% in your mid-70s, ~5% around 80, and higher from there. This rising schedule is why a large pre-tax balance can produce ballooning taxable income late in retirement, dragging Social Security into tax and tripping IRMAA. You can always take more than the RMD; you just can't take less.

The penalty (now less brutal). Historically, missing an RMD carried a punishing 50% excise tax on the amount you failed to withdraw. SECURE 2.0 cut that to 25%, and to just 10% if you correct the shortfall within a short correction window and file the right form. Still painful — and easily avoided by taking the distribution before December 31 (or, for your first RMD only, by April 1 of the following year).

The first-year timing trap. Your very first RMD can be deferred to April 1 of the year after you turn 73. Tempting — but if you wait, you must take two RMDs in that second year (the delayed first one plus that year's), potentially spiking your income, your bracket, your Social Security taxation, and your IRMAA all at once. Usually it's better to take the first RMD in the year you turn 73 and avoid the double-up.

Roth accounts escape entirely. Roth IRAs have never had RMDs for the original owner. And as of 2024, SECURE 2.0 eliminated RMDs from Roth 401(k)s too. This is a major planning point: every dollar you move into Roth (via conversions in your 60s) is a dollar removed from the RMD machine — it won't force taxable income later, won't tax your Social Security, and won't push you toward an IRMAA tier.

QCDs: turn a tax bill into a gift. If you're 70½ or older, a Qualified Charitable Distribution lets you send money directly from your IRA to a qualified charity — up to about $108,000 in 2026 (indexed annually) — and it counts toward your RMD while being excluded from your taxable income entirely. That's better than taking the RMD and then donating: a QCD lowers your AGI directly, which can also help with Social Security taxation and IRMAA. For charitably inclined retirees, it's one of the most efficient moves available.

Worked example. A 75-year-old has $800,000 in a traditional IRA and a ~24.6 life-expectancy factor, giving an RMD of about $32,500 he doesn't need for spending. Taken as income, it lands on top of his Social Security and could nudge his MAGI toward an IRMAA line. Instead he directs $20,000 of it to his church as a QCD — satisfying that portion of the RMD with zero addition to his taxable income — and takes the remaining $12,500 as cash. He meets the requirement, supports his charity, and keeps his AGI (and Medicare premiums) lower.

Aggregation rules (a practical detail). If you have multiple IRAs, you can total their RMDs and take the whole amount from any one of them. But 401(k)s must each satisfy their own RMD separately — you can't take a 401(k)'s RMD from an IRA or vice versa. Get this wrong and you can accidentally under-withdraw.

Key action items

  • Know your RMD age. 73 if born 1951–1959; 75 if born 1960 or later.
  • Take the first RMD on time — usually in the year you turn 73. Avoid the April-1 deferral that forces two RMDs in one year.
  • Shrink the balance early. Use gap-year Roth conversions and drawdowns to reduce the pre-tax pile before RMDs begin.
  • Use QCDs if you give. From 70½, route charitable gifts straight from your IRA to cut AGI and satisfy RMDs tax-free.
  • Respect aggregation rules. IRAs can be combined; each 401(k) stands alone.
  • Never miss the deadline. Take it by December 31; a missed RMD is a 25% (or 10% if promptly fixed) penalty.
