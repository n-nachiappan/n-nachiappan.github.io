Home Equity as Retirement Income

Summary

  • For most retirees, home equity is the largest asset they own — often bigger than the whole investment portfolio. So leaving it untouched isn't automatically the right call.
  • Three ways to turn it into income without simply moving: a reverse mortgage, downsizing, or a sale-leaseback — the workhorse being the reverse mortgage.
  • The main product is a federally insured HECM (Home Equity Conversion Mortgage, age 62+).
  • You draw from your equity, make no monthly payments, and the loan is repaid when you sell, move out, or die.
  • You can take it as a lump sum, a growing line of credit, term payments, or tenure payments — a fixed monthly income for as long as you live in the home.
  • Tenure is the closest thing to an annuity funded by your house.
  • The sophisticated use isn't income at all: a standby line of credit as a buffer against sequence-of-returns risk — draw on it in down markets so you don't sell investments low.
  • A big hidden advantage: reverse-mortgage proceeds are a loan, not taxable income — they don't raise your MAGI, tax your Social Security, or trip IRMAA (Chapter 12); the price is the fees and the debt that compounds against your equity.

The fourth bucket. This guide spends most of its time on three buckets — taxable, tax-deferred, and tax-free. For a great many retirees there's a fourth that dwarfs them: the house. Americans 62+ hold well over $14 trillion in home equity, yet most plans treat it as untouchable — a legacy asset, or a last resort. That's a defensible choice, but it should be a choice, not a reflex. If you're "house-rich and cash-poor," or you want to defer Social Security, or you want a shock absorber for bad markets, your home can do real work. There are three ways to tap it without giving up the life you have: borrow against it (a reverse mortgage), sell and buy smaller (downsize), or sell and stay (sale-leaseback).

The reverse mortgage, and why the HECM is the one that matters. A reverse mortgage lets a homeowner 62 or older convert equity into cash while keeping the title and living in the home. You make no monthly payments; instead the balance grows as interest and fees accrue, and it's repaid — usually from the sale of the home — when the last borrower sells, moves out for more than 12 months, or dies. The dominant product is the HECM, insured by the FHA, and its government backing buys three protections a private reverse mortgage may not: mandatory independent counseling, capped fees, and — crucially — a non-recourse guarantee. Non-recourse means neither you nor your heirs can ever owe more than the home is worth: if the balance grows past the home's value, FHA insurance covers the gap, and heirs settle by paying the lesser of the balance or 95% of the appraised value. You keep the title throughout — but you remain responsible for property taxes, homeowners insurance, and upkeep, and falling behind on those can trigger foreclosure.

The payout options — including the “home-equity annuity.” A HECM can pay you in several shapes, and the shape is the whole decision:

- Lump sum — one fixed-rate draw. Simplest, but you start paying interest on the whole amount immediately, and a first-year limit caps most borrowers at 60% of the available principal.
- Line of credit (LOC) — borrow only what you use, when you use it. The most flexible option — and the basis for the buffer strategy below.
- Term — equal monthly payments for a set number of years.
- Tenure — equal monthly payments for as long as you live in the home, however long that is. This is the annuity analog: the lender divides your available principal by a factor that assumes you live to about 100, and if you outlive that, the payments keep coming and FHA absorbs the cost. Like a SPIA (Chapter 16), it's longevity insurance — but funded by your house, non-recourse, and with no medical underwriting.
- Modified term / tenure — a line of credit plus a monthly payment, blending flexibility and steady income.

The smart move: a standby line of credit. The most compelling use of a HECM, in the research, isn't day-one income at all. Open a line of credit early and leave it untouched: the unused portion grows every year at the loan's interest rate plus the insurance premium, so your available credit quietly compounds into a large, guaranteed liquidity reserve later in life — exactly when you may need it. Then use it as a buffer against sequence-of-returns risk (Chapter 8): in a year the market falls, spend from the credit line instead of selling depressed investments, and let the portfolio recover. Wade Pfau and other researchers have shown that coordinating draws this way — for example, tapping the line when your portfolio dips below its planned path — can meaningfully improve how long the money lasts without sacrificing the legacy you leave. It flips the reflex "tap the house last" into "open the house early, use it strategically."

Reverse Mortgage taxation. Because a reverse mortgage is a loan, the money you receive is not taxable income. That's not a footnote — it's a lever. Reverse-mortgage draws don't raise your Adjusted Gross Income, so they don't push more of your Social Security into tax (the "tax torpedo," Chapter 11), and they don't count toward the MAGI that sets your IRMAA tier (Chapter 12). In a year you'd otherwise be forced to realize a lot of taxable income — or when you're a hair under an IRMAA cliff — funding spending from tax-free home equity instead of an IRA withdrawal can save real money. Used deliberately, the house becomes a tax-management tool, not just a source of cash.

The costs, and what to watch out for. None of this is free, and reverse mortgages have earned their cautious reputation:

- Upfront and ongoing costs. Expect an FHA mortgage-insurance premium of 2% of the home's value up front plus 0.5%/year on the balance, an origination fee (capped by formula, up to about $6,000), plus appraisal and closing costs. On smaller draws these costs loom large.
- Compounding debt erodes your equity. With no payments, interest capitalizes; the balance climbs and the equity — and any inheritance in the home — shrinks over time.
- You must keep up the property charges. Property taxes, insurance, and maintenance remain yours. Miss them and the loan can be called due; lenders run annual occupancy checks.
- Moving out ends it. If you leave for assisted living for more than 12 months, the loan comes due — so it's a poor fit if a move is likely soon.
- Means-tested benefits. A large lump sum can push you over the asset limits for Medicaid or SSI; a line of credit or monthly draws usually avoid this, but plan carefully.
- Protect a non-borrowing spouse. A younger spouse must be named in the loan as an eligible non-borrowing spouse to stay in the home after the borrower dies — and using the younger age lowers how much you can borrow. Get this right in the paperwork.
- Sales pressure and scams. Seniors are targeted with aggressive pitches. A classic red flag: anyone urging you to use reverse-mortgage proceeds to buy an annuity or insurance policy. That cross-sell is inappropriate — and the mandatory HUD counseling exists precisely to give you an independent check before you sign.

The alternatives: downsize, or sell and stay. If you're open to moving, downsizing is often the cleanest way to unlock equity: sell the big house, buy or rent something smaller and cheaper, and invest the difference — or annuitize part of it (Chapter 16) for guaranteed income. A married couple can typically exclude up to $500,000 of home-sale gain from tax ($250,000 single), which makes this very tax-efficient. A sale-leaseback (or "home reversion") sits in between: you sell the home — or a share of it — for cash and lease it back so you can stay put, trading ownership and future appreciation for liquidity now. These avoid a growing loan balance, but you give up the home (and its step-up in basis for heirs, Chapter 26).

Worked example

A standby line of credit

Diane, 70, owns a paid-off $600,000 home and has $500,000 invested. Rather than wait for a crisis, she opens a HECM line of credit with roughly $300,000 available and draws nothing at first — the line grows a few percent a year. She keeps deferring Social Security toward 70 and lives off her portfolio. At 74 the market falls 25%; instead of selling stocks down, she funds that year's spending with a tax-free $45,000 draw from the credit line, letting her portfolio recover — and, because the draw isn't income, she stays comfortably under her IRMAA threshold. Later she converts part of the remaining line to tenure payments for a guaranteed monthly floor. If the balance ever exceeds the home's value, non-recourse means she and her heirs owe no more than the house is worth.

Key action items

  • Count the house in the plan. Treat home equity as a real resource — a buffer, an income source, or a deliberate legacy — not an afterthought.
  • If aging in place, open the line early. A HECM line of credit opened at 62–70 and left to grow is far more valuable than one opened in a crisis.
  • Use tax-free draws strategically. Lean on home equity in down-market years and to stay under IRMAA cliffs — not for discretionary splurges.
  • Read the real costs. Weigh the 2% upfront insurance, ongoing fees, and compounding interest against the benefit; do the mandatory counseling and shop lenders. And treat any “use the proceeds to buy an annuity” pitch as a red flag — walk away.
  • Protect your spouse and your home. Name a non-borrowing spouse, and be honest about whether you can keep up taxes, insurance, and upkeep for the long haul.
  • Compare downsizing. If you'd move anyway, selling and investing (or annuitizing) the proceeds — using the home-sale exclusion — may beat borrowing.
