Employer Plans: The 401(k) and Its Cousins

Summary

  • The workplace retirement plan — a 401(k), or its cousins the 403(b) (nonprofits/schools), 457(b) (government), and TSP (federal employees) — is the backbone of most Americans' retirement savings, mostly because contributions come straight out of your paycheck before you can spend them.
  • The employer match is free money. Contributing at least enough to capture the full match is the highest-return move in personal finance; skipping it is leaving part of your salary on the table.
  • For 2026 you can defer up to $24,500 of salary, plus a $8,000 catch-up at 50+, or a $11,250 "super catch-up" at ages 60–63. Counting employer contributions, total additions can reach $72,000.
  • You usually choose pre-tax (deduct now, taxed in retirement) or Roth (taxed now, tax-free later) contributions — a bet on whether your tax rate is higher today or in retirement.
  • Watch vesting (employer money may not be fully yours until you've stayed a few years) and, starting 2026, a SECURE 2.0 rule requiring high earners' catch-ups to go into Roth.
  • When you leave a job, your 401(k) doesn't leave with your employer — you can keep it, roll it to an IRA, or move it to a new plan. That choice has real consequences later (see Chapters 12 and 19).

Why the 401(k) is the workhorse. A 401(k) is an employer-sponsored, tax-advantaged account funded by automatic payroll deferrals. Two features make it powerful: the money is taken out before it hits your checking account (so you never "feel" it and can't easily spend it), and it grows tax-sheltered for decades. The 403(b), 457(b), and federal TSP work almost identically — same spirit, minor rule differences — so everything here largely applies to all of them.

The match: the closest thing to free money. Many employers match a portion of what you contribute — a common formula is 50% or 100% of your contributions up to 3–6% of pay. If your employer matches 100% up to 6% and you contribute 6%, you just earned an instant 100% return on that money before any market gains. The rule that follows is simple and nearly universal: contribute at least enough to get the entire match before you fund anything else. Not doing so is declining part of your compensation.

The 2026 limits. The IRS sets annual caps (Notice 2025-67 for 2026): - Employee deferral: $24,500. - Catch-up (age 50+): an extra $8,000, for $32,500 total. - "Super catch-up" (ages 60–63): an extra $11,250 instead of the $8,000, for $35,750 total — a SECURE 2.0 provision, if your plan offers it. - Combined employee + employer limit: $72,000 (the ceiling on all additions to your account, including match and any after-tax contributions).

Pre-tax vs. Roth: the core choice. Most plans let you contribute pre-tax, Roth, or a blend: - Pre-tax (traditional): contributions reduce this year's taxable income; withdrawals in retirement are taxed as ordinary income. Best when you expect a lower tax rate in retirement. - Roth 401(k): contributions are made with after-tax dollars (no deduction now); qualified withdrawals are entirely tax-free. Best when you expect a higher or similar future rate — and, unlike a Roth IRA, a Roth 401(k) has no income limit, so high earners can use it. As of 2024, Roth 401(k)s also escaped required minimum distributions.

A reasonable default for younger or lower-bracket savers is Roth (lock in today's low rate); for peak-earning-years savers in high brackets, pre-tax often wins. Many people split the difference.

Vesting — your money vs. their money. Your own contributions are always 100% yours immediately. Employer contributions may be subject to a vesting schedule — you might need to stay two to six years before the match is fully yours. If you're thinking of leaving a job, check your vesting; walking away a few months early can forfeit thousands.

Two rules worth flagging. First, highly compensated employees (earning $160,000+ in 2026) can face plan-testing limits on how much they defer. Second, a SECURE 2.0 change effective 2026 requires anyone whose prior-year wages from the employer exceeded $150,000 to make their catch-up contributions on a Roth basis — you still get the catch-up, but lose the upfront deduction on it (and if your plan offers no Roth option, you may lose the ability to make catch-ups at all).

The mega backdoor (a bonus for some plans). A minority of plans allow after-tax contributions beyond the $24,500 deferral, up to the $72,000 total limit, which you can then convert to Roth — the "mega backdoor Roth" (Chapter 3). If your plan supports it and you have income to spare, it's a way to shovel large sums into Roth.

When you leave. A 401(k) stays yours after you leave the employer. Your options: leave it in the old plan, roll it into your new employer's plan, or roll it to an IRA. Rolling to an IRA opens up more investment choices and often lower fees — but, as Chapter 19 warns, it can also forfeit the Rule of 55 (penalty-free access at 55+). Don't roll reflexively; decide based on your age and access needs.

Key action items

  • Capture the full match first — always. Contribute at least up to the match before any other investing.
  • Push toward the limit as you can. Aim to increase deferrals over time toward the $24,500 (plus catch-ups) cap.
  • Choose pre-tax vs. Roth deliberately. Base it on today's bracket vs. your expected retirement bracket; splitting is fine.
  • Use the super catch-up at 60–63. If you're in that window and your plan allows it, the $11,250 catch-up is a late-career booster.
  • Mind vesting before you quit. Confirm how much employer money is actually yours.
  • Don't roll to an IRA on autopilot when you leave. Weigh the Rule-of-55 tradeoff (Chapter 19) first.
