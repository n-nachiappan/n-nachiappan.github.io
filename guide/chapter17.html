Withdrawal Sequencing Across Account Types

Summary

  • You typically hold three kinds of accounts — taxable (brokerage), tax-deferred (traditional 401(k)/IRA), and tax-free (Roth).
  • The order you spend them in changes your lifetime tax bill, sometimes dramatically.
  • The classic rule of thumb — spend taxable first, then tax-deferred, then Roth last — is a decent default but often leaves money on the table.
  • The smarter approach is tax-bracket-aware sequencing: instead of emptying one bucket before touching the next, pull from whichever combination fills up your low brackets each year without spilling into high ones.
  • Sequencing isn't a standalone decision — it's the hub that connects Roth conversions (Ch 12), Social Security timing (Ch 13), RMDs (Ch 18), and IRMAA (Ch 11).
  • The prize is real: thoughtful sequencing can add years of portfolio longevity versus a naive order, with no change to how much you spend or how you invest.

Why order matters at all. Each bucket is taxed differently on the way out. Taxable accounts owe capital-gains tax only on growth (often at preferential 0/15/20% rates), and get a step-up in basis at death. Tax-deferred withdrawals are fully ordinary income. Roth withdrawals are tax-free and never forced. Because these rates differ and because tax brackets are progressive, which dollars you pull — and when — determines how much the IRS takes over your whole retirement.

The conventional rule of thumb. The textbook order is: spend taxable first, let tax-deferred and Roth keep compounding; then tax-deferred; then Roth last. The logic is to preserve tax-advantaged growth as long as possible and save the tax-free bucket for the end. It's a reasonable default and better than spending randomly. But it has a flaw: by leaving the traditional IRA untouched for years, it lets that balance grow into large RMDs at 73, which can then erupt into a high bracket, tax your Social Security, and trip IRMAA — the very spike Chapter 12 warns about. The naïve order can minimize taxes early and maximize them late.

The better way: fill the brackets every year. Rather than emptying buckets in strict sequence, aim to realize roughly the same, moderate amount of taxable income each year — smoothing your rate across retirement instead of letting it swing from near-zero to very high. In practice that often means: in low-income gap years, deliberately pull from (or convert) the tax-deferred account to fill up the 10%/12% brackets — even if the rule of thumb says leave it alone — while using taxable and Roth dollars to top off spending without adding taxable income. You're voluntarily paying a little tax in cheap years to avoid a lot in expensive ones. This is the same insight as Roth conversions, applied to spending: the enemy is a lumpy tax-rate profile.

Worked example

Two retirees, same spending

Two retirees each spend $70,000/year from a mix of $600k traditional, $300k taxable, and $200k Roth. Retiree A follows the strict rule of thumb: taxable first. For years his taxable income is tiny (just the gains on brokerage sales), so he "wastes" his low brackets — then at 73 his still-large traditional IRA throws off big RMDs that land him in the 22%+ zone with taxed Social Security. Retiree B instead pulls ~$30k/year from the traditional IRA even while spending taxable dollars, filling the 12% bracket every year. By 73 her traditional balance — and thus her RMDs — is far smaller, keeping her in low brackets and under IRMAA for life. Same spending, same investments; B keeps materially more.

Coordinate with the other levers. Sequencing is where the whole plan comes together: - Social Security timing (Ch 13): the gap years before you claim are your best low-rate window — lean on tax-deferred withdrawals or conversions then. - RMDs (Ch 18): once they start, RMDs are mandatory taxable income; sequence around them, not into a pile-up. - IRMAA (Ch 11) and the tax torpedo (Ch 10): every sequencing choice moves your MAGI, so watch the two-year IRMAA lookback and Social Security taxation thresholds. - Legacy: taxable assets get a step-up in basis at death and Roth passes tax-free, so if leaving money to heirs matters, that shifts which buckets you preserve.

Don't over-optimize into fragility. The goal is a smooth, sensible rate profile, not squeezing the last dollar. Rules change, markets surprise, and life is lumpy. A robust plan that keeps you in low-to-middle brackets most years beats a hyper-tuned one that assumes everything goes to script.

Key action items

  • Inventory the three buckets and their basis. You can't sequence what you haven't mapped.
  • Aim for a smooth taxable-income profile. Target filling low brackets every year rather than emptying one bucket at a time.
  • Use gap years aggressively. Pull from or convert tax-deferred money while your rate is low.
  • Sequence around RMDs and Social Security, not into them. Draw down the pre-tax balance before RMDs force it at a bad time.
  • Check MAGI effects every year. Confirm your withdrawal mix doesn't trip IRMAA or the Social Security torpedo.
  • Let legacy goals inform the tail. Preserve step-up-eligible taxable assets and tax-free Roth for heirs if that's a priority.
