Passing Assets to Your Heirs: Beneficiaries, TOD, and Avoiding Probate

Summary

  • Chapter 26 covered how inherited assets are taxed. This chapter covers the other half: how title actually passes — and how to keep it out of probate, the public, months-long, sometimes costly court process that governs anything with no other route.
  • Most assets can pass outside your will and outside probate if you set them up to. There are four main tools: beneficiary designations, payable-on-death (POD) and transfer-on-death (TOD) registrations, TOD deeds / joint title for real estate, and a revocable living trust.
  • Retirement accounts, life insurance, and annuities pass by beneficiary designation — a contract that overrides your will. Name a primary and a contingent beneficiary on every one, and keep them current.
  • Bank accounts take a POD beneficiary; brokerage accounts take a TOD registration. Both skip probate and cost nothing to add.
  • Real estate can pass by a transfer-on-death (beneficiary) deed in most states, by joint tenancy with right of survivorship, or through a trust — each with trade-offs.
  • A revocable living trust avoids probate (including ancillary probate on out-of-state property), keeps your affairs private, and manages assets if you're incapacitated — but only for assets you actually retitle into it.
  • Everyone still needs a will (it names an executor and a guardian for minor children), and a trust needs a pour-over will to sweep in anything you forgot to retitle.
  • The through-line: coordinate everything. Designations, deeds, trust, and will must point the same direction — because the designations win.

Probate is the default — and the thing to route around. When you die, every asset you own has to get to someone. Probate is the court-supervised process that does it for anything with no other instruction attached: the court validates your will (or, if you have none, applies your state's intestacy formula), appoints an executor, notifies creditors, and eventually distributes what's left. It works — but it is public (your will and inventory become court records), slow (commonly six months to well over a year), and in some states expensive (attorney and court fees can run a few percent of the estate). Own property in a second state and your family may face a second, ancillary probate there. Almost all the planning in this chapter is about letting assets pass without touching probate at all — faster, private, and cheaper. Crucially, avoiding probate is a separate question from avoiding tax: a probate-avoiding transfer still gets the step-up or the ordinary-income treatment from Chapter 26. You want both handled.

Retirement accounts pass by beneficiary designation — and it beats your will. Your IRA, 401(k), 403(b), life insurance, and annuities are not controlled by your will. They pass by contract to whoever is named on the beneficiary form — and that form overrides everything else, including a later will that says otherwise. This is the single most common estate-planning failure: an ex-spouse still listed on a 401(k), a form left blank so the account defaults to the estate (dragging it into probate and, for an IRA, often collapsing the heirs' payout window). Fixes are simple but must be done deliberately:

• Name a primary and a contingent beneficiary on every account, so the asset still has somewhere to go if your first choice predeceases you.
• Choose "per stirpes" if you want a deceased beneficiary's share to flow to their children rather than being split among the survivors.
• Know the 401(k) spousal rule: under federal law (ERISA), your spouse is automatically the beneficiary of a workplace plan unless they sign a written, notarized waiver. IRAs have no such rule (outside community-property states), so an IRA can quietly name someone other than a spouse.
• Don't name a minor directly — a child can't legally receive the account, forcing a court-appointed guardian or custodian. Use a custodial (UTMA) arrangement or a trust instead.
• Be careful naming a trust as an IRA beneficiary: it must be a properly drafted "see-through" trust to preserve the 10-year payout, and a special-needs trust is the right tool for a disabled heir who would otherwise lose benefits.

Bank accounts: payable-on-death (POD). Almost any checking, savings, or CD can carry a POD beneficiary — sometimes called a "Totten trust" or "in trust for" account. You keep full control while alive (the beneficiary has no access and can't touch it), and at death the money passes directly to the named person on presentation of a death certificate, skipping probate entirely. It costs nothing to add at the bank and is revocable any time. It's the simplest probate-avoidance tool in existence — and widely underused.

Brokerage and investment accounts: transfer-on-death (TOD). The brokerage equivalent is a TOD registration (authorized in nearly every state under the Uniform TOD Security Registration Act). Register a taxable brokerage account, or individual securities, as TOD and the holdings move to your named beneficiaries outside probate — while you retain complete control to trade, withdraw, or change the designation during life. One caution: a TOD/POD designation is a blunt instrument. It pays a fixed share to a named person on a date certain; it can't say "in equal shares to my children, but hold my son's part until he's 30." When you need conditions, staggered timing, or protection for a vulnerable heir, that's the job of a trust, below.

Real estate: three ways to skip probate. A home is often the largest probate asset, and there are three routes around it:

• Transfer-on-death (beneficiary) deed. Most states now let you record a TOD deed (also called a beneficiary deed) that names who inherits the property at death, with no effect on your ownership or control while you're alive — you can sell, mortgage, or revoke it freely, and the heir gets a full step-up in basis. It's the cleanest option where available. A handful of states instead offer the similar "Lady Bird" (enhanced life estate) deed.
• Joint tenancy with right of survivorship (and, for spouses, tenancy by the entirety). The property passes automatically to the surviving co-owner. Simple, but it has real downsides: adding a non-spouse as joint owner is a gift that can forfeit part of the step-up, exposes the property to the co-owner's creditors and divorce, and gives away control — you can no longer sell or refinance without their signature.
• A living trust holds title to the home and passes it per the trust terms — the best choice when you own property in more than one state (it avoids a second, ancillary probate) or want conditions on the transfer.

Other assets — the odds and ends. Many states allow TOD registration for vehicles and even boats at the DMV; some allow TOD for small-business interests. A modest "tangible personal property" list referenced by your will can direct furniture, jewelry, and heirlooms without itemizing them in the will itself. And most states offer a small-estate affidavit that lets heirs collect modest leftover assets without full probate. Whatever doesn't have a beneficiary, a TOD/POD tag, joint title, or a trust to carry it will pass through your will — and therefore through probate.

The revocable living trust — the probate-avoidance workhorse. A revocable living trust is a container you create during life, name yourself as trustee of, and move your assets into. Because you keep full control and can change or revoke it at will, it changes nothing about your taxes — the assets are still yours, still in your estate, and still get the step-up. What it buys you is process:

• Probate avoidance. Anything titled in the trust passes to your beneficiaries per the trust terms with no court involvement — privately and quickly.
• Incapacity planning. If you become unable to manage your affairs, your named successor trustee steps in immediately — no court-supervised conservatorship.
• Control and conditions. Unlike a blunt TOD tag, a trust can stagger distributions ("half at 25, half at 30"), protect a spendthrift or special-needs heir, or hold assets for minor children.
• Multi-state ease. Out-of-state real estate in the trust avoids a separate ancillary probate.

The catch is that a trust only governs assets you actually retitle into it. An unfunded trust — the classic mistake — is an empty box; the assets you never moved still go through probate. Funding it means changing the deed on your home, retitling brokerage and bank accounts to the trust, and (carefully) reviewing which beneficiary designations should name the trust versus a person. Note that a revocable trust does not avoid estate tax or shield assets from creditors while you're alive — those require different, irrevocable structures and are only worth the complexity for the few families above the exemption from Chapter 26.

You still need a will — and, with a trust, a pour-over will. A trust doesn't replace a will. A will is the only document that can name an executor and, most importantly, name a guardian for minor children — no trust or beneficiary form can do that. Die without one (intestate) and your state's default formula decides who gets what, and a judge decides who raises your kids. When you have a living trust, you pair it with a pour-over will: a short companion will that acts as a safety net, catching any asset you forgot to retitle and directing it into the trust at death. (Those swept-in assets still pass through probate first — the pour-over is a backstop, not a substitute for funding the trust properly.)

The whole toolkit, and the golden rule. Think of it as layers: beneficiary designations and POD/TOD tags handle accounts; a TOD deed or trust handles real estate; a trust adds control, privacy, and incapacity protection; and a will (pour-over, if you have a trust) names your executor and guardians and catches the rest. Above all: coordinate them. The most expensive estate mistakes come from documents that contradict each other — a will leaving the IRA "equally to my children" while the beneficiary form still names an ex-spouse. The form wins. Review every designation, deed, and document together after every marriage, divorce, birth, death, or move.

Worked example

Two estates, same size, very different endings

Robert and Susan each die with a $900,000 estate: a $400k home, a $300k IRA, a $150k brokerage account, and $50k in the bank. Neither owes estate tax. What differs is how it's titled.

Asset ($900k total)Robert — nothing set upSusan — designations + trust
Home ($400k)Passes by will → probateTOD deed / in trust → skips probate
IRA ($300k)Form left blank → to estate, into probateNames spouse + contingent kids → direct
Brokerage ($150k)Individual account → probateTOD registration → direct
Bank ($50k)Individual account → probatePOD beneficiary → direct

Robert's heirs wait a year or more, pay court and attorney fees on the full $900k, and see the whole estate become a public record — and his blank IRA form may collapse the heirs' payout window. Susan's family gets nearly everything within weeks, privately, with no probate — the same dollars, the same tax result, a completely different experience. The difference cost her a few beneficiary forms, a deed, and one afternoon with an attorney.

Key action items

  • Audit every beneficiary form. On each IRA, 401(k), annuity, and life-insurance policy, confirm a primary and a contingent beneficiary — and that neither is an ex-spouse or someone deceased. These override your will.
  • Add POD/TOD to your accounts. Tag bank accounts payable-on-death and brokerage accounts transfer-on-death. It's free, revocable, and skips probate.
  • Set up your home to pass outside probate. Record a TOD/beneficiary deed where your state allows it, or hold the home in a living trust — especially if you own property in more than one state.
  • Get (or update) a will. Everyone needs one to name an executor and a guardian for minor children. Don't leave it to intestacy.
  • Consider a revocable living trust if you value privacy, want conditions on distributions, own out-of-state property, or want seamless incapacity coverage — and if you set one up, fund it (retitle the assets) and add a pour-over will.
  • Don't name minors directly as beneficiaries; use a custodial account or trust. Use a special-needs trust for a disabled heir.
  • Coordinate everything, and add powers of attorney (financial and healthcare) so someone can act for you while you're alive. Re-review after every major life event.
