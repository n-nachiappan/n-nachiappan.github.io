Part V · Beyond the Money — The Good Life · Chapter 30

Handing Over the Reins: Directives, Simplification, and Legacy

Summary

  • Every plan eventually needs a co-pilot. This chapter sets up — while you're fully capable — the documents and simplifications that let a trusted person act for you if a day comes when you can't.
  • Five documents do the work, in two buckets. Health: a living will / advance directive and a health-care proxy (durable power of attorney for health care). Money & legal: a durable financial power of attorney, a will, and often a revocable living trust (Ch 27).
  • The durable financial power of attorney is the one people forget — it's what lets someone pay your bills and manage your accounts while you're alive but incapacitated, when a will does nothing.
  • Consolidate and simplify while you're sharp: roll old 401(k)s together, close duplicate accounts, and audit beneficiary designations (they override the will — Ch 27). Fewer accounts means simpler RMDs, easier oversight as you age, and a far easier estate to settle.
  • Add a FINRA "trusted contact" to your brokerage accounts. Diminished capacity and elder fraud are exactly why you set these protections up before you need them.
  • Assemble one findable "legacy folder" — account list, documents, advisors, digital passwords, and your care and funeral wishes — and tell your proxy where it is.

The plan needs a co-pilot. Everything so far assumes a competent you at the controls — making the withdrawals, filing the taxes, working the strategy. But retirements run for decades, and the ability to manage money is often one of the first things to slip. The purpose of this chapter is to build the handoff while you're fully capable, so that if illness, an accident, or simple aging takes you out of the pilot's seat, a person you chose can step in smoothly — instead of your family going to court to get permission. Do this early and it's an afternoon of paperwork. Skip it and the default is a court-supervised guardianship: slow, public, expensive, and decided by a judge who has never met you.

The health documents. Two papers (sometimes combined into one "advance directive") cover medical decisions if you can't speak for yourself:

• Living will. Your written instructions about the care you would and wouldn't want — life support, resuscitation, feeding tubes, comfort care — when you can't communicate.
• Health-care proxy (a durable power of attorney for health care). Names the person who makes medical decisions for you — your agent or surrogate. A proxy covers the situations a living will never anticipated, which is why most people want both.
• Supporting pieces: a HIPAA authorization so your proxy and family can actually access your medical information, and — for the seriously ill — a POLST/MOLST, a medical order that travels with you across care settings.

Treat these as living documents: review them at least yearly and after any major change (a move to a new state, a new diagnosis, retirement itself), and give copies to your proxy, your doctors, and your family. A directive nobody can find does nothing.

The financial handoff: the durable power of attorney. This is the most-forgotten document in retirement, and the one whose absence causes the most avoidable pain. A durable financial power of attorney authorizes someone to manage your money — pay bills, file taxes, manage investments, handle the house — if you become unable to. "Durable" is the crucial word: it means the authority survives your incapacity, which is the entire point. (A springing POA activates only once you're declared incapacitated; an immediate one is effective on signing — each has trade-offs worth discussing with an attorney.) Note the division of labor: a will and beneficiary forms only operate after death; a durable POA is the only one of these tools that lets someone act for you during life. Without it, no spouse or child can legally touch an account in your sole name — they must petition a court for guardianship first.

Simplify while you can — for you now, and for your heirs later. Financial complexity that's merely annoying at 65 becomes dangerous at 85 and a burden to whoever settles your estate. Three moves pay off twice:

• Consolidate accounts. Roll old 401(k)s from past employers into a single IRA; collapse duplicate brokerage and bank accounts. Fewer accounts means simpler RMDs (Ch 20), cleaner withdrawal sequencing (Ch 19), fewer statements to monitor for fraud, and far less for an executor to hunt down. The classic estate nightmare is the forgotten account nobody knew existed.
• Audit every beneficiary designation. They override your will (Ch 27). Confirm a primary and a contingent on each account, and that no ex-spouse or deceased person is still listed.
• Add POD/TOD tags to bank and brokerage accounts so they pass directly and skip probate (Ch 27).

Guard against diminished capacity and fraud. Older adults are the prime target for financial exploitation, and the defenses are most effective when installed before they're needed. Name a "trusted contact" on each brokerage account: under FINRA rules your firm can reach this person — and place a temporary hold on suspicious withdrawals for up to 15 business days — if it suspects exploitation or that you've lost capacity. A trusted contact can't trade or control the account; they're an emergency contact, not an agent, which makes them safe to name. And for whoever eventually steps in as your agent or fiduciary, the CFPB's free "Managing Someone Else's Money" guides are the plain-English manual for doing it honestly and well.

The legacy folder. The last gift you leave isn't in any account — it's findability. Assemble one secure, clearly labeled place (physical, digital, or both) that your proxy and executor know about and can reach, containing: a list of all accounts and institutions; the five documents above (or where the originals live); your advisors (attorney, CPA, financial advisor) and their contacts; insurance policies; the digital estate — a password manager or list, so email, photos, and online accounts aren't lost behind a login nobody can crack; and your wishes for care and for a funeral or memorial. The National Institute on Aging's "Getting Your Affairs in Order" checklist is a ready-made template. Some people add an ethical will — a short letter of values, stories, and gratitude — the one document heirs treasure most and the tax code cares about least.

Same dollars, same love for the same family — one afternoon of paperwork apart. Tom's estate cost his children a year of their lives; Raj's cost them a phone call and a folder. The reins are worth handing over on purpose, while your hands are still steady.

Key action items

  • Sign the three core documents now. A living will, a health-care proxy, and a durable financial power of attorney — the last is the one most people skip and most need.
  • Consolidate. Roll old 401(k)s into a single IRA and close stray accounts — simpler RMDs, easier oversight, a cleaner estate.
  • Add a trusted contact to every brokerage account. It costs nothing and lets your firm intervene against fraud or a bad decision if your judgment slips.
  • Audit beneficiary forms and add POD/TOD tags. They override the will (Ch 27); confirm primary and contingent on each, and no stale names.
  • Build the legacy folder. Account list, documents, advisors, digital passwords, and care/funeral wishes — in one findable place your proxy knows about.
  • Re-review after every major life event, and distribute copies. Give directives to your proxy and doctors; refresh everything after a move, marriage, divorce, death, or diagnosis.
  • Have the conversation. Tell your proxy and family your actual wishes out loud — the document names the person, but the talk is what lets them act without guilt or guesswork.
