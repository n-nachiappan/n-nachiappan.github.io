Life Expectancy Tables (RMDs & SEPP)

The idea

  • Nearly every forced or early distribution — an RMD or a 72(t)/SEPP payment — is computed by dividing an account balance by a life-expectancy factor from an IRS table.
  • Three tables do the work: the Uniform Lifetime Table (most owners taking RMDs), the Single Life Expectancy Table (beneficiaries of inherited accounts, and one of the SEPP methods), and the Joint Life and Last Survivor Table (owners whose sole beneficiary is a spouse more than 10 years younger).
  • The bigger the factor, the smaller the required withdrawal — so a younger beneficiary or spouse lowers the forced draw.
  • These are the current (post-2021) tables, which were lengthened to reflect longer life expectancies, slightly reducing RMDs across the board.

Read the factor as "the number of years the IRS spreads your balance over." Your withdrawal for the year is:

Withdrawal = prior Dec 31 balance ÷ life-expectancy factor

Which table you use depends on who you are relative to the account:

3 · Joint Life and Last Survivor Table

This one is a full two-way grid — one factor for every combination of your age and your spouse's age — so it's far too large to reproduce here (see IRS Pub 590-B, Table II). You only need it in one situation: your spouse is the sole beneficiary of the account for the whole year and is more than 10 years younger than you. Then the joint factor is larger than the Uniform Lifetime factor, which lowers your RMD.

A useful anchor: the Uniform Lifetime Table is the joint table computed for a beneficiary exactly 10 years younger. So a spouse 10 years younger gives you the same factor as the Uniform table; a wider age gap gives you a bigger factor (smaller RMD). Illustrative factors for a 73-year-old owner:

How these tables feed a 72(t) / SEPP plan

Early retirees using Rule 72(t) to draw penalty-free before 59½ compute their Substantially Equal Periodic Payment with one of three IRS-approved methods. Two of them use exactly the tables above; the third uses an annuity factor. Under current guidance (IRS Notice 2022-6) you may choose an interest rate up to the greater of 5% or 120% of the federal mid-term rate, and you may use any of the three life tables.

SEPP methodHow the payment is setPayment
Required Minimum DistributionBalance ÷ life-expectancy factor (Uniform, Single, or Joint table), recalculated every year. No interest-rate assumption.Lowest; varies with the balance
Fixed AmortizationBalance amortized over the life-expectancy factor at your chosen interest rate. Computed once.Higher; fixed for the whole schedule
Fixed AnnuitizationBalance ÷ an annuity factor built from an IRS mortality table at your chosen interest rate. Computed once.Higher; fixed for the whole schedule

The tradeoff: the RMD method gives the smallest, most flexible payment (it moves with the market); the two fixed methods lock in a larger, steady payment. You may make a one-time switch from either fixed method to the RMD method if a falling balance threatens to bust the plan — a built-in escape hatch. Whatever you pick, the schedule must run for the longer of 5 years or until age 59½, or the penalties come back retroactively.

Factors reflect the current IRS tables effective for distribution years 2022 and later.
