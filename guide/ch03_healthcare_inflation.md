# Chapter 3 — Healthcare Inflation and How to Plan for It

### Summary of key points

- Healthcare is the retirement expense that behaves worst: it's large, it inflates faster than everything else, and it arrives unevenly — modest for years, then suddenly enormous.
- Fidelity's 2025 estimate is that a single 65-year-old retiring today will spend about **$172,500** (after tax) on healthcare over retirement — roughly **$345,000 for a couple** — and that figure explicitly *excludes* long-term care.
- Medical costs have historically risen about **5%–6% a year**, well above general inflation of ~3%. That gap compounds viciously over a 30-year retirement.
- Medicare is the foundation, not the whole house. It leaves real gaps — premiums, deductibles, coinsurance, dental, vision, hearing, and most long-term care.
- The single most powerful pre-retirement tool is the **HSA**, the only triple-tax-advantaged account in the code. The single biggest wildcard is **long-term care**, which roughly 70% of 65-year-olds will need in some form.
- Plan healthcare as its *own* line item with its *own* (higher) inflation rate — don't blend it into one household number.

### Details

**The size of the problem.** Fidelity has published an annual retiree healthcare estimate for over two decades. In 2025 it reached **$172,500** for a single 65-year-old — a lifetime, after-tax figure covering Medicare premiums, deductibles, coinsurance, and out-of-pocket drug costs, but *not* long-term care. For a couple it's about **$345,000**. For perspective, Fidelity's first estimate in 2002 was $80,000; the number has more than doubled, rising over 4% in the past year alone. This isn't a scare statistic — it's a planning input. If you don't have a healthcare line in your retirement budget, you're implicitly assuming one of your largest lifetime expenses is zero.

**Why it inflates faster than everything else.** General inflation runs around 3% over the long run; medical care has historically run **5%–6%**. That means healthcare costs roughly *double* every 12–14 years, versus every ~24 for general prices. Over a 30-year retirement, a category growing at 5.5% while your income grows at 2.8% (the Social Security COLA) becomes a larger and larger share of your budget every single year. This is the mechanism behind the "retiree inflation gap" from Chapter 2 — healthcare's weight is *why* older households experience higher inflation.

**What Medicare does and doesn't cover.** At 65 you become eligible for Medicare, which is essential but incomplete. Part A (hospital) is premium-free for most; Part B (outpatient/physician) carries a standard premium of **$202.90/month in 2026** plus 20% coinsurance with no out-of-pocket cap under Original Medicare; Part D covers drugs with its own premium and cost-sharing. Higher earners pay IRMAA surcharges on top (Chapter 5). What Medicare largely *doesn't* cover: routine dental, vision, hearing aids, and — critically — long-term custodial care. Retirees fill these gaps with a Medigap (supplement) policy or a Medicare Advantage plan, plus out-of-pocket spending.

**The HSA: your best pre-retirement weapon.** A Health Savings Account is the only account with a *triple* tax advantage: contributions go in pre-tax, growth is tax-free, and withdrawals for qualified medical expenses are tax-free. For 2026 you can contribute **$4,400** (self-only) or **$8,750** (family), plus a **$1,000** catch-up if you're 55+. Two underused facts: first, you can treat an HSA as a stealth retirement account — pay small medical bills out of pocket while young, let the HSA compound for decades, and reimburse yourself later. Second, after 65 you can withdraw HSA funds for *any* purpose penalty-free (you just pay ordinary income tax on non-medical use, exactly like a traditional IRA), while medical use — including Medicare Part B and D premiums — stays tax-free. The main caveat: you can't contribute to an HSA once enrolled in Medicare, so the window is *before* 65.

**The long-term care wildcard.** This is the expense that breaks plans. About **70%** of people reaching 65 will need some form of long-term care, and costs can run five or six figures *per year* for a home health aide, assisted living, or a nursing home. Fidelity's $172,500 figure deliberately excludes it. Your options to cover it are essentially four: self-fund from savings, qualify for Medicaid (after spending down assets), buy traditional long-term-care insurance (cheapest when purchased in your 50s, before health issues arise), or use a hybrid life/LTC policy. There's no clean answer, but ignoring the question is the one guaranteed-wrong choice.

*Worked example — the compounding gap.* Suppose healthcare is $12,000 of an $80,000 first-year budget. If your general expenses grow at 2.8% but healthcare grows at 5.5%, then in 20 years general expenses are about $20,800 while the healthcare slice has grown to about $35,000. What was 15% of your budget becomes closer to 22% — and every dollar of it competes with the rest of your spending. Modeling one blended inflation rate hides this entirely.

### Key action items

- **Give healthcare its own budget line and its own inflation rate.** Assume ~5–6% for the medical slice, not the household average.
- **Max the HSA while you still can.** Contribute before Medicare enrollment; consider paying current medical bills out of pocket and letting the HSA grow.
- **Plan the pre-65 bridge.** If you retire before 65, budget for ACA-marketplace or COBRA coverage until Medicare starts — it can be expensive.
- **Choose your Medicare structure deliberately.** Original Medicare + Medigap (predictable, flexible providers) vs. Medicare Advantage (lower premiums, network limits). Decide before your enrollment window.
- **Make a long-term-care decision — even if the decision is "self-fund."** If insuring, price it in your 50s. If self-funding, earmark a reserve.
- **Watch enrollment deadlines.** Late Medicare enrollment triggers *permanent* premium penalties.

> **🧭 Model it.** Because the calculator lets you set inflation and see how spending outpaces income over time, use it to pressure-test a healthcare shock: raise your spending target in the later years to simulate a long-term-care event, and watch how it interacts with RMDs and IRMAA (a big withdrawal can push you into a surcharge tier). Seeing the tax-and-Medicare ripple of a late-life care cost is exactly the kind of thing that's invisible in a simple spreadsheet.
