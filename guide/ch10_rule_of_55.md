# Chapter 10 — The Rule of 55

### Summary of key points

- The "Rule of 55" lets you take penalty-free withdrawals from your **current employer's 401(k) or 403(b)** if you **leave that job in or after the calendar year you turn 55** — no 10% early-withdrawal penalty.
- It's far more **flexible** than 72(t): no fixed schedule, no multi-year commitment. Take what you want, when you want.
- It applies **only to the plan at the employer you just left** — not to IRAs, and not to old 401(k)s from previous jobs.
- The classic trap: **rolling your 401(k) into an IRA first** destroys the Rule-of-55 option, because IRAs don't qualify.
- Qualified **public-safety workers** (police, firefighters, EMS, etc.) get an even earlier version, at age **50**.
- For a 55-to-59½ retiree with a workplace plan, this is usually the simplest bridge to penalty-free access — often simpler than 72(t).

### Details

**What the rule actually says.** Ordinarily, withdrawals from a 401(k) before 59½ carry a 10% penalty. The Rule of 55 (an IRS provision, sometimes called the "separation from service" exception) waives that penalty on distributions from the plan of the employer you *separate from*, as long as the separation happens in or after the year you reach age 55. You still owe ordinary income tax — the rule removes only the *penalty*. Timing is by calendar year: if you turn 55 at any point in the year you leave, you qualify, even if you actually separate in, say, March before your birthday.

**Why it's easier than 72(t).** Compared to the Rule of 72(t) (Chapter 9), the Rule of 55 is dramatically less rigid. There's no required payment schedule, no locked five-year commitment, and no "busting" penalty for taking the wrong amount. You simply withdraw from the plan as needed — a lump sum, occasional draws, or a steady stream, whatever your plan permits. That flexibility makes it the preferred bridge for people who retire (or are laid off) at 55 or later and have a workplace plan to draw on.

**The critical limitations.** Three constraints define the rule, and missing any one is costly:
1. **Only the most-recent employer's plan.** The exception applies to the 401(k)/403(b) of the job you just left. Old 401(k)s from prior employers *don't* qualify — unless you first roll them *into* your current plan while still employed (if the plan allows), consolidating them under the qualifying plan before you separate.
2. **Not IRAs.** IRAs are never eligible for the Rule of 55. This is the big one.
3. **Your plan must permit partial/flexible withdrawals.** Some employer plans force a full lump-sum distribution rather than allowing periodic withdrawals after separation. Check your plan's rules *before* you rely on this.

**The rollover trap.** The single most common and expensive mistake: separating from service at 55+, then rolling the 401(k) into an IRA (a natural-seeming move for more investment choices and lower fees) — which *forfeits* the Rule of 55, because the money is now in an IRA. If you'll need penalty-free access between 55 and 59½, **leave the money in the 401(k)** until you're past 59½, then roll it over. Consider a partial approach: keep enough in the 401(k) to fund your bridge years and roll the rest to an IRA if you prefer.

**The public-safety head start.** Qualified public-safety employees — police officers, firefighters, EMTs/paramedics, air-traffic controllers, certain federal law-enforcement and customs personnel — get the exception at age **50** (or after 25 years of service under the plan, per SECURE 2.0 enhancements) rather than 55, reflecting the earlier retirement norms in those careers.

*Worked example.* An engineer is laid off in the year she turns 56. She has $700,000 in her current employer's 401(k) and $200,000 in an old IRA. Under the Rule of 55, she can pull from the $700,000 401(k) penalty-free (paying only income tax) to fund the years until 59½ — taking flexible amounts as needed. Her instinct is to roll the whole 401(k) to an IRA for better funds; if she does, she loses penalty-free access and would have to fall back on a rigid 72(t). Instead she leaves the 401(k) in place as her bridge, taps it as needed to 59½, and rolls it to an IRA afterward.

### Key action items

- **Check the calendar-year rule.** You qualify if you separate in or after the year you turn 55 (50 for qualified public-safety workers).
- **Don't roll to an IRA yet.** If you need penalty-free access before 59½, leave the money in the 401(k).
- **Consolidate first if helpful.** While still employed, consider rolling old 401(k)s *into* your current plan so more money qualifies.
- **Confirm your plan allows flexible withdrawals.** Some plans force a lump sum — verify before counting on periodic draws.
- **Compare with 72(t).** If you have a qualifying workplace plan, the Rule of 55 is usually simpler; use 72(t) when you only have IRAs or separate before 55.
- **Mind the tax bracket.** Withdrawals are ordinary income — size them to avoid needlessly spiking your rate (Chapters 4 and 8).
