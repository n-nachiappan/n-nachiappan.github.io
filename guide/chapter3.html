Roth Accounts and the Backdoor

Summary

  • A Roth account flips the usual deal: you pay tax on the money going in, then it grows and comes out completely tax-free — and a Roth IRA has no required minimum distributions for you, ever.
  • Roth is most valuable when you expect your future tax rate to be as high or higher than today's — younger savers, lower-bracket years, and anyone who wants tax-free flexibility and a tax-free inheritance for heirs.
  • Roth IRAs have income limits (2026 phase-out: $153,000–$168,000 single, $242,000–$252,000 joint). Earn more and you can't contribute directly — but the backdoor Roth gets you in anyway.
  • The backdoor Roth: contribute to a non-deductible traditional IRA, then convert it to Roth. Simple in theory, but the pro-rata rule can tax it if you hold other pre-tax IRA money — plan around that.
  • The mega backdoor Roth (if your 401(k) allows after-tax contributions) can move tens of thousands more into Roth each year.
  • Mind the 5-year rules and, on the workplace side, the 2026 SECURE 2.0 rule that pushes high earners' catch-ups into Roth.

What makes Roth special. With a traditional account you get a tax break now and pay later; with a Roth you skip the break now and pay nothing later. Beyond tax-free growth, a Roth IRA has two standout features: no lifetime RMDs (traditional accounts force taxable withdrawals starting at 73 — Roth IRAs never do), and tax-free inheritance (heirs pay no income tax on Roth dollars, though they must generally empty the account within 10 years — Chapter 26). Those features make Roth a uniquely flexible bucket in retirement and a powerful estate-planning tool.

When Roth wins. The core question is your tax rate now vs. later. Roth is favored when you're in a low bracket today (early career, a gap year, a low-income stretch) and expect higher rates later, when you want a tax-free source to draw from in retirement to manage brackets and IRMAA (Chapter 11), or when leaving a tax-free legacy matters. Traditional tends to win in your peak-earning, high-bracket years. Most people benefit from holding both — "tax diversification" — so they can choose which bucket to tap each year in retirement.

The two flavors. A Roth IRA you open yourself (subject to income limits below). A Roth 401(k) is the Roth option inside your workplace plan — no income limit, much higher contribution room, and (as of 2024) no RMDs. High earners locked out of a Roth IRA can still use a Roth 401(k) directly.

The Roth IRA income limits. You can contribute the full $7,500 to a Roth IRA only if your income is below the phase-out, which for 2026 runs $153,000–$168,000 (single) and $242,000–$252,000 (married filing jointly). In the phase-out range you can contribute a reduced amount; above it, zero — directly. That's where the backdoor comes in.

The backdoor Roth. For high earners over the income limit, the backdoor is a legal two-step: (1) contribute up to $7,500 to a non-deductible traditional IRA (allowed at any income), then (2) convert that traditional IRA to a Roth IRA. Because the contribution was already after-tax, the conversion is (ideally) tax-free, and you've funded a Roth despite exceeding the income limit.

The pro-rata trap. The catch that snares people: the IRS treats all your traditional IRA money as one pool when you convert. If you hold other pre-tax IRA balances (say, a large rollover IRA from an old 401(k)), your conversion is taxed pro-rata across pre-tax and after-tax dollars — you can't cherry-pick only the new after-tax contribution. Example: with $93,000 of pre-tax IRA money and a fresh $7,000 non-deductible contribution, 93% of any conversion is taxable. The fix is usually to roll pre-tax IRA balances into a 401(k) first (401(k)s aren't counted in the pro-rata math), leaving only after-tax money in the IRA to convert cleanly.

The mega backdoor Roth. If your employer plan allows after-tax contributions (distinct from Roth deferrals) and in-plan Roth conversions or withdrawals, you can contribute well beyond the $24,500 deferral — up to the $72,000 combined limit — and convert the after-tax portion to Roth. For high earners with cash to spare and the right plan, this shovels large sums into tax-free growth each year. Not all plans support it; check yours.

The 5-year rules (don't trip on them). Two separate clocks: (1) your Roth IRA earnings are tax-free only after the account has been open five years and you're 59½; (2) each conversion has its own five-year clock before you can withdraw the converted principal penalty-free if you're under 59½. Contributions (not earnings) can always come out tax- and penalty-free. Keep records of contributions vs. conversions.

One 2026 workplace note. Under SECURE 2.0, starting 2026, employees earning over $150,000 (prior-year wages) must make 401(k) catch-up contributions as Roth. Not a conversion, but part of the broad shift toward Roth — and a reason high earners should confirm their plan offers a Roth option.

Key action items

  • Prioritize Roth in low-bracket years. Early career, gap years, and low-income stretches are ideal for Roth contributions.
  • Hold both Roth and traditional. Tax diversification lets you manage brackets and IRMAA in retirement.
  • Use the backdoor if you're over the income limit — carefully. Non-deductible contribution, then convert.
  • Clear out pre-tax IRA money first. Roll it into a 401(k) before a backdoor to avoid the pro-rata tax.
  • Explore the mega backdoor. If your plan allows after-tax contributions plus conversions, it's a large Roth opportunity.
  • Track your 5-year clocks and basis. Keep contribution/conversion records so withdrawals stay penalty-free.
