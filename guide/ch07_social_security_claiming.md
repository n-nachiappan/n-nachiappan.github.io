# Chapter 7 — When to Claim Social Security

### Summary of key points

- Social Security is an inflation-adjusted, government-backed annuity for life. *When* you claim — anywhere from 62 to 70 — permanently sets its size, and the difference is large.
- Claiming at **62 cuts your benefit by 30%** (for a full retirement age of 67); waiting to **70 raises it to 124%** of your full benefit. Each year of delay past full retirement age adds about **8%**.
- The naïve way to decide is a **breakeven** age (usually late 70s to low 80s). The better way is **longevity insurance**: delaying protects you against the expensive risk of living a long time.
- For married couples, the higher earner's claim age sets the **survivor benefit** — the amount the surviving spouse keeps for life. This usually makes delaying by the higher earner the most valuable move in the whole decision.
- Benefits get an annual **COLA** (2.8% for 2026), tied to CPI-W. Claiming early also runs into the **earnings test** if you're still working.
- The right answer is rarely "claim as early as possible." For most people with decent health and any longevity in the family, *delaying the higher earner's benefit* is the strongest play.

### Details

**How the claim-age dial works.** Your **Primary Insurance Amount (PIA)** is the benefit you'd get at your **full retirement age (FRA)** — 67 for anyone born in 1960 or later. Claim before FRA and the benefit is reduced (5/9 of 1% per month for the first 36 months early, then 5/12 of 1% per month beyond that), so claiming at 62 with an FRA of 67 lands you at **70% of PIA — a permanent 30% cut**. Claim *after* FRA and you earn delayed retirement credits of **2/3 of 1% per month (8% per year)** up to age 70, where the benefit maxes at **124% of PIA**. There's no reward for waiting past 70. In 2026 dollars, the maximum benefit runs from about **$2,969/month at 62** to about **$5,181/month at 70** — the same worker, nearly double the check, based only on timing.

**Breakeven vs. longevity insurance.** The common framing is: "If I delay, I give up checks now; when do the bigger later checks add up to more?" That breakeven usually falls somewhere in the **late 70s to low 80s**. If that's all you consider, delaying looks like a coin flip on how long you'll live. But that framing has it backwards. Social Security is best understood not as an investment to optimize but as *insurance against outliving your money*. The scenario that wrecks retirements isn't dying early — it's living to 95 with a depleted portfolio. Delaying buys a larger, inflation-protected, government-guaranteed income for exactly that expensive scenario. You don't buy fire insurance hoping your house burns; you buy it because the downside is ruinous. Same logic here.

**The survivor benefit — the couple's trump card.** For married couples, the decision isn't about one life; it's about two, and especially about the second death. When one spouse dies, the survivor keeps the *larger* of the two benefits, not both. That means the **higher earner's** claim age sets the income the survivor will live on for the rest of their life. If the higher earner delays to 70, they lock in the biggest possible survivor benefit — protection that lasts through *both* lifetimes, and is most valuable precisely when the surviving spouse is oldest and most vulnerable. A frequently optimal pattern: the **higher earner delays to 70**; the **lower earner claims earlier** to bring in some cash flow; short life expectancy pushes toward claiming early. This asymmetry is why "just take it at 62" is so often the wrong call for couples.

**COLA and the earnings test.** Benefits rise each year with a cost-of-living adjustment — **2.8% for 2026** — tied to CPI-W (which, as Chapter 2 notes, may understate retiree inflation). Note the COLA applies to your PIA whether or not you've claimed, so delaying doesn't cost you inflation protection. One more wrinkle: if you claim before FRA *and* keep working, the **earnings test** withholds $1 of benefits for every $2 earned above **$24,480** (2026); in the year you reach FRA the limit jumps to **$65,160** with a gentler $1-per-$3 withholding. Withheld benefits aren't lost forever — they're restored via a higher benefit later — but the test is a reason not to claim early while still earning meaningful income.

**When claiming early does make sense.** Delaying isn't universal. Claim earlier if you have a serious health condition or short life expectancy, if you're single with no survivor to protect and modest longevity prospects, or if claiming lets you avoid draining an investment account during a bad market (a form of sequence-risk management). The point isn't "always wait" — it's to make the choice on longevity and survivor logic, not on the fear of "leaving money on the table."

### Key action items

- **Get your numbers.** Pull your PIA and benefit estimates at 62, FRA, and 70 from your Social Security statement.
- **Frame it as longevity insurance, not a breakeven bet.** Weight the decision toward protecting the long-life scenario.
- **Coordinate as a couple.** Strongly consider having the higher earner delay to 70 to maximize the survivor benefit; let the lower earner claim earlier if cash flow is needed.
- **Mind the earnings test.** If you'll keep working before FRA, delaying often avoids benefit withholding.
- **Use delay years for Roth conversions.** The pre-claim window is ideal for the conversions in Chapter 6 — before benefits complicate your taxable income.
- **Factor in health honestly.** Short life expectancy is a legitimate reason to claim early.
