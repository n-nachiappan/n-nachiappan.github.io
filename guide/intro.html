Retirement Is Three Problems, Not One

The idea

  • Almost everything you've read about retirement is about one of its three problems — how to build the savings. This guide gives equal weight to the two that follow: how to spend the pile down without running out, and how to pass on what's left.
  • The spend-down half is the hard, under-taught part — a tangle of interacting rules (the 4% rule, Social Security timing, Medicare surcharges, taxes, required distributions) that no single book usually covers in one place. This one does.
  • Every chapter pairs with a free drawdown calculator, so you can stop reading about a decision and actually model it against your own numbers.

Why this matters. The financial industry has spent decades teaching people how to accumulate: contribute to the 401(k), capture the match, buy the index fund, repeat. That advice is good — and it's the easy half. The moment you stop working, the problem inverts. Now you have a finite pile of money that has to produce a paycheck for a retirement that might last thirty years or more, through markets you can't predict, a tax code you now partly control, healthcare costs that rise faster than everything else, and a lifespan nobody can quote you in advance. Get the accumulation phase wrong and you arrive with less. Get the decumulation phase wrong and you can run out — or, just as commonly, die with a fortune you were too afraid to spend. This is where the real money is made and lost, and it's the part almost no one is taught.

Three problems, not one. It helps to see retirement as three distinct problems, each with its own logic and its own mistakes:

ProblemPhaseWhat it asksCovered in
BuildAccumulationSave in the right accounts, in the right order — capturing every match and tax break, and letting tax-advantaged compounding do the work.Part I
SpendDecumulationTurn the pile into a paycheck that lasts — net of taxes, Medicare, inflation, market luck, and a long life.Parts II–III
Pass onLegacyLeave what you don't spend the tax-smart way — the step-up and Roth to your heirs, the pre-tax accounts to yourself.Part IV

Most planning obsesses over the first row. This guide is deliberately weighted toward the second — because that's the row where the decisions are hardest, the stakes are highest, and good advice is scarcest.

What this guide covers

The book runs in five numbered parts, roughly in the order life hands the decisions to you — preceded by the Foundations and followed by a capstone and a reference shelf. The last part turns from the money to the non-financial side of retirement.

How to use this guide

Each chapter is built the same way, so you can move fast or go deep:

  • A Summary box up top — the whole chapter in a few bullets, if that's all you need.
  • The main text, with a worked example wherever a number makes the idea concrete.
  • A Key action items list — what to actually do.
  • A Model it in the calculator box — a specific scenario to run against your own numbers.
  • References & sources — the IRS, CMS, SSA, and research each figure comes from.

Read it your way. Front-to-back gives you the whole arc from first paycheck to final legacy. But the chapters are self-contained, so you can also jump to the decision in front of you — the contents and the glossary both link straight to it. If you want the big picture first, read the capstone and come back for the detail. In a hurry, the summaries and action items alone are a real education.

Where to start, by life stage

The guide is written to be read in order, but the decision that matters most to you depends on where you are. Use this as an express route: find the row that fits, read those chapters first, then widen out. The ages are rough guides, not gates — and if you're planning as a couple, you may each sit in a different row.

If you're…What matters most right nowStart with
In your 20s–30s
Getting started		 Build the habit and open the right accounts. Your single biggest advantage is time — a dollar saved now outruns several saved later. The Foundations (how much to save, mortgage, how to invest), then Part I — the 401(k) match, Roth, and the HSA. See F4 for why starting early wins.
In your 40s
Peak accumulation		 Max the right accounts and build tax diversification across pre-tax, Roth, and taxable — the buckets you'll spend from later. All of Part I, plus the mortgage question. Start skimming Part II so you fill today's buckets with tomorrow's spend-down in mind.
50 to mid-50s
The on-ramp		 Catch-up contributions open at 50, and the Rule of 55 bridge appears. Time to sketch the spend-down plan. The catch-up rules (Ch 1–2), then the core of Part II — the 4% rule, inflation, healthcare, and withdrawal sequencing.
Retiring before 59½
Early exit		 Two gaps to bridge: reaching your money without the 10% penalty, and covering health insurance until Medicare at 65. All of Part III — 72(t)/SEPP, the Rule of 55, and the ACA bridge — plus sequence-of-returns risk.
Late 50s to mid-60s
The transition		 The low-tax window between your last paycheck and your first RMD — the years the biggest tax levers are in your hands. Roth conversions, when to claim Social Security, IRMAA, and sequencing. Confirm the age milestones you're about to hit.
Newly retired (65+)
Executing the plan		 Turning the pile into a paycheck — and getting Medicare, claiming, and the tax sequence right from day one. Part II end-to-end (especially healthcare, IRMAA, claiming), the risk chapters woven through Part II, and Part V · Beyond the Money.
73 and beyond
Deep retirement & legacy		 RMDs force withdrawals, and attention turns to longevity and what you pass on. RMDs & SECURE 2.0, longevity risk, Part IV (inheritance), and housing and handing over the reins in Part V.

Whichever row you start in, the capstone ties the whole plan together, Appendix A lays out every age-triggered deadline on one timeline, and the 2026 numbers live in Appendix B.
