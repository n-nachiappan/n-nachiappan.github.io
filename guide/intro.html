Retirement Is Three Problems, Not One

The idea

  • Almost everything you've read about retirement is about one of its three problems — how to build the savings. This guide gives equal weight to the two that follow: how to spend the pile down without running out, and how to pass on what's left.
  • The spend-down half is the hard, under-taught part — a tangle of interacting rules (the 4% rule, Social Security timing, Medicare surcharges, taxes, required distributions) that no single book usually covers in one place. This one does.
  • Every chapter pairs with a free drawdown calculator, so you can stop reading about a decision and actually model it against your own numbers.

Why this matters. The financial industry has spent decades teaching people how to accumulate: contribute to the 401(k), capture the match, buy the index fund, repeat. That advice is good — and it's the easy half. The moment you stop working, the problem inverts. Now you have a finite pile of money that has to produce a paycheck for a retirement that might last thirty years or more, through markets you can't predict, a tax code you now partly control, healthcare costs that rise faster than everything else, and a lifespan nobody can quote you in advance. Get the accumulation phase wrong and you arrive with less. Get the decumulation phase wrong and you can run out — or, just as commonly, die with a fortune you were too afraid to spend. This is where the real money is made and lost, and it's the part almost no one is taught.

Three problems, not one. It helps to see retirement as three distinct problems, each with its own logic and its own mistakes:

ProblemPhaseWhat it asksCovered in
BuildAccumulationSave in the right accounts, in the right order — capturing every match and tax break, and letting tax-advantaged compounding do the work.Part I
SpendDecumulationTurn the pile into a paycheck that lasts — net of taxes, Medicare, inflation, market luck, and a long life.Parts II–IV
Pass onLegacyLeave what you don't spend the tax-smart way — the step-up and Roth to your heirs, the pre-tax accounts to yourself.Part V

Most planning obsesses over the first row. This guide is deliberately weighted toward the second — because that's the row where the decisions are hardest, the stakes are highest, and good advice is scarcest.

How to use this guide

Each chapter is built the same way, so you can move fast or go deep:

  • A Summary box up top — the whole chapter in a few bullets, if that's all you need.
  • The main text, with a worked example wherever a number makes the idea concrete.
  • A Key action items list — what to actually do.
  • A Model it in the calculator box — a specific scenario to run against your own numbers.
  • References & sources — the IRS, CMS, SSA, and research each figure comes from.

Read it your way. Front-to-back gives you the whole arc from first paycheck to final legacy. But the chapters are self-contained, so you can also jump to the decision in front of you — the contents and the glossary both link straight to it. If you want the big picture first, read the capstone and come back for the detail. In a hurry, the summaries and action items alone are a real education.

Use the calculator. A retirement plan is a set of assumptions until you test it. The companion drawdown calculator turns the decisions in these chapters — your withdrawal rate, Roth conversions, when to claim Social Security, how to sequence your accounts — into a result you can see and compare. Reading tells you what the levers are; the calculator tells you what they do to your numbers.

Model it in the calculator

Before you read another word, open the calculator in a second tab and put in your real balances, ages, and spending. You don't need to understand every input yet — just get your own numbers on screen. Every chapter from here will point you to the levers that move the result.
