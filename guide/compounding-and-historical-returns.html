The Power of Compounding and Historical Returns

Summary

  • Compounding — earning returns on your prior returns — is the engine behind every retirement plan; over decades it turns modest sums into large ones and small rate differences into huge outcome differences.
  • Over the long run (1928–2025), U.S. stocks returned about 10% nominal / ~6.5–7% real per year; long-term Treasuries about 4.9%; T-bills about 3.3%; inflation about 3%.
  • The scale is hard to intuit: $100 invested in U.S. stocks in 1928 grew to roughly $1.16 million by the end of 2025 — that's compounding.
  • History is reassuring on patience: no rolling 20-year period in that record produced a negative total stock return.
  • Time in the market smooths the terror of any single year.
  • But you can't plan on averages alone — once you're drawing the money down, the order of returns matters (Chapter 8), and today's high valuations may mean lower future returns than history's averages.
  • Diversification, low costs, and staying invested are the practical levers.
  • The math rewards patience and punishes panic.

What compounding actually does. Compounding means your gains start generating their own gains. A dollar earning 7% becomes $1.07 after year one — but the next year's 7% is on $1.07, not $1.00, and so on. The effect is slow at first and then startling: at ~7% real, money roughly doubles every decade. Over a 30-year retirement (plus the decades of saving before it), this exponential curve is why starting early and staying invested matter more than almost any clever tactic.

The long-run numbers. Drawing on the widely cited data assembled by NYU's Aswath Damodaran for 1928–2025, the long-run annual averages look like this: U.S. large-cap stocks ~10% nominal, which after ~3% inflation is roughly 6.5–7% real; 10-year Treasuries ~4.9%; T-bills ~3.3%; inflation ~3%. Stocks pay the most and swing the most; bonds and bills pay less but steady the ride. The "equity risk premium" — the extra return stocks deliver over bonds for bearing their volatility — is the reward that funds most long retirements.

The scale of it. Numbers this abstract are easier to feel with a concrete case: $100 put into U.S. stocks in 1928 would have grown to about $1.16 million by the end of 2025, dividends reinvested. The same $100 in Treasury bills would be a few thousand dollars. That roughly thousand-fold difference over a lifetime is the equity premium compounding across a century — and the reason retirees, who often have 30+ year horizons, generally shouldn't abandon stocks entirely.

Patience beats timing. Any single year can be terrifying — stocks have dropped 30–50% in bad ones. But stretch the window and the picture calms dramatically: across the 1928–2025 record, no rolling 20-year holding period produced a negative total return for U.S. stocks. Even the worst 20-year stretches ground out gains. The practical implication for a retiree with a long horizon: the money you won't spend for 15–20+ years can weather volatility that would be reckless for near-term spending — which is exactly the logic behind the cash/bond buffer for the early years (Chapter 8) and equities for the later ones.

Two honest caveats. First, averages hide sequence risk. A 7% average with the bad years first can still sink a retiree who's withdrawing (Chapter 8). Retirement math cares about the path, not just the destination. Second, the future may not match the past. After a long bull market, U.S. stock valuations (measured by the Shiller CAPE) sit well above historical norms, and bond yields are lower than their century average. Forward-looking models (Morningstar, Vanguard) therefore project returns below the historical averages — which is why their safe-withdrawal numbers (Chapter 7) come in lower than Bengen's backward-looking ones. History is the base rate, not a guarantee.

The practical levers. You can't control returns, but you can control three things that reliably improve outcomes: diversification (spreading across asset classes and geographies so no single bet sinks you), cost (every basis point of fees is a permanent drag on compounding — low-cost index funds keep more of the return), and behavior (staying invested through downturns rather than selling at the bottom, which is where most real-world investors lose to the averages). Compounding does the heavy lifting; your job is mostly to not interrupt it.

Worked example

The cost of a small drag

Two retirees each start with $1,000,000 earning 7% before fees. One pays 0.1% (index funds); the other pays 1.1% (higher-cost funds). After 25 years the low-cost investor has about $5.0M and the high-cost investor about $3.9M — a ~$1.1M gap created purely by a 1% annual fee compounding. Same market, same returns; costs alone reshaped the outcome.

Key action items

  • Let time work — start and stay invested. The biggest compounding gains come from the years you don't interrupt.
  • Anchor expectations to real returns. Plan on ~6–7% real for stocks historically, but consider a haircut for today's high valuations.
  • Keep a long-horizon bucket in stocks. Money you won't touch for 15–20+ years can ride out volatility that no 20-year window has made permanent.
  • Cut costs ruthlessly. Favor low-cost, diversified funds; fees are a guaranteed subtraction from compounding.
  • Diversify and rebalance. Don't bet the retirement on one asset; rebalancing enforces buy-low/sell-high.
  • Don't confuse averages with your path. Pair this chapter's optimism with Chapter 8's caution about sequence.
