Rule of 72(t): Early Penalty-Free Access

Summary

  • Normally, withdrawing from an IRA or 401(k) before age 59½ triggers a 10% early-withdrawal penalty on top of income tax.
  • Rule 72(t) is the main legal escape hatch: it lets you take Substantially Equal Periodic Payments (SEPP) — a fixed schedule of withdrawals — penalty-free, at any age, from an IRA (and sometimes other plans).
  • There are three IRS-approved calculation methods: Required Minimum Distribution (smallest, recalculated yearly), Fixed Amortization (larger, fixed), and Fixed Annuitization (fixed, uses a mortality table).
  • The commitment is strict: you must continue for five years or until you reach 59½, whichever is longer.
  • Break it and the IRS retroactively applies the 10% penalty to every payment, plus interest.
  • Recent interest-rate rules (Revenue Ruling 2022-6) let you use a rate up to the greater of 5% or 120% of the federal mid-term rate, which meaningfully raised allowable payouts.
  • 72(t) is powerful but rigid — for committed early retirees who need steady access and can live with a locked schedule.

The problem it solves. If you retire in your early-to-mid 50s (or earlier), you may need to tap retirement accounts before 59½. The 10% penalty makes that painful. Section 72(t)(2)(A)(iv) of the tax code carves out an exception: if you commit to a SEPP schedule, the penalty disappears (you still owe ordinary income tax on the withdrawals). It's the classic FIRE-movement tool for bridging the years before penalty-free access.

The three methods. All three compute an annual payment from your account balance, your age (via an IRS life-expectancy table), and an interest rate. They differ in size and flexibility: - Required Minimum Distribution method — recalculated each year off your current balance. It produces the smallest payment and one that varies year to year (rising and falling with the account). Choose it if you want the lowest mandatory draw. - Fixed Amortization method — calculated once and then fixed for the whole schedule, like a loan amortization. Produces a larger, level payment. The most common choice when you need more income. - Fixed Annuitization method — also fixed, but derived using an IRS annuity/mortality factor. Similar in spirit to amortization; the numbers differ slightly.

You also choose among three life-expectancy tables (Single Life gives the largest payment, Uniform Lifetime a middle one, Joint Life and Last Survivor the smallest), which lets you tune the payout somewhat.

The rate rules that changed the math. The size of an amortization or annuitization payment is very sensitive to the interest rate you're allowed to use. Under Revenue Ruling 2022-6, you may use any rate up to the greater of 5% or 120% of the federal mid-term rate. That 5% floor was a big deal: when market rates were near zero, SEPP payments were tiny; the floor now lets early retirees pull meaningfully more. In mid-2025 conditions, allowable rates supported payouts of roughly 5.2%–5.4% of the dedicated balance.

Worked example

How big is the SEPP payment?

Ben, age 55, dedicates a $500,000 IRA to a 72(t) plan. He uses the Single Life factor for age 55 — 31.6 (Appendix C) — and the allowable 5% interest rate. The same balance produces very different annual payments depending on method:

MethodHow it's computedAnnual SEPP
RMD$500,000 ÷ 31.6$15,823
Fixed Amortizationamortize $500,000 over 31.6 yrs @ 5%$31,807
Fixed Annuitization$500,000 ÷ annuity factor @ 5%≈ $32,000

The amortization figure is the loan-style formula $500,000 × 0.05 ÷ (1 − 1.05−31.6) = $31,807. Note the range: the same $500,000 legally supports anywhere from ~$15,800 to ~$31,800 a year — a 2× spread — purely by method choice.

Ben needs about $30,000, so he picks amortization and is locked into exactly $31,807 every year for the longer of 5 years or until 59½ — here 5 years wins (to age 60), since 59½ is only 4½ years away. If that were more than he needed, he'd dedicate a smaller IRA rather than ever take a different amount: deviating by a single dollar busts the plan and claws back the 10% penalty on every prior withdrawal.

The iron commitment — and "busting." This is the part that punishes mistakes. Once you start a SEPP, you must continue exactly the calculated payments (no more, no less) for the longer of five years or until you turn 59½. So a 52-year-old is locked in until 59½ (about 7½ years); a 58-year-old is locked in for a full five years, to 63. If you deviate — take an extra dollar, stop early, or roll the account improperly — you "bust" the plan, and the IRS retroactively assesses the 10% penalty on all prior SEPP withdrawals, plus interest. That can be a five-figure surprise. The IRS does allow a one-time switch to the (smaller) RMD method if the fixed payment becomes unsustainable — a built-in relief valve worth knowing.

How to reduce the risk. The standard defensive move is to split your IRA before starting: dedicate just enough to one IRA to generate the SEPP payment you need, and leave the rest in a separate IRA untouched. That way, if you later need more money, you can start a second 72(t) on the reserve IRA (or access it another way after 59½) without busting the first. Never commingle SEPP and non-SEPP funds in the same account.

Worked example

Splitting an IRA before a SEPP

A 54-year-old with a $500,000 IRA needs about $22,000/year to bridge to 59½. Rather than lock up the whole account, she carves out ~$420,000 into a dedicated IRA and runs a fixed-amortization SEPP on it at an allowable ~5.2% rate, producing roughly $22,000/year. She must take exactly that amount every year until 59½ (5½ years). The remaining ~$80,000 sits in a separate IRA as a reserve she can tap penalty-free after 59½ or via a fresh SEPP if her needs change — protecting her from busting the first plan.

Key action items

  • Confirm you actually need it. If you have taxable savings or a Rule-of-55 option (Chapter 22), those may be simpler. Use 72(t) when you need penalty-free IRA access before 55–59½.
  • Split the IRA first. Dedicate only the balance required to generate your target payment; keep a separate reserve.
  • Pick the method for your income need. Amortization/annuitization for larger fixed payments; RMD method for the smallest, flexible draw.
  • Model the payment before committing. Small changes in balance, age, or rate move the payment; get it right up front.
  • Never deviate. Take the exact amount, on schedule, until the longer of 5 years or 59½. Know the one-time switch to the RMD method exists as relief.
  • Get professional help. A busted 72(t) is expensive; this is a place to confirm the math with a CPA.
