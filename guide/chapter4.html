The HSA: Your Stealth Retirement Account

Summary

  • A Health Savings Account is the only account in the tax code with a triple tax advantage: money goes in pre-tax, grows tax-free, and comes out tax-free for qualified medical expenses.
  • To contribute you must be covered by a high-deductible health plan (HDHP) and not enrolled in Medicare.
  • For 2026 the limits are $4,400 (self-only) and $8,750 (family), plus a $1,000 catch-up at 55+.
  • The power move is to treat the HSA as a stealth retirement account: pay small medical bills out of pocket while working, invest the HSA, and let it compound for decades.
  • After age 65, non-medical withdrawals are penalty-free (just taxed like a traditional IRA).
  • Medical withdrawals, including Medicare Part B and D premiums, stay tax-free.
  • The catch: you can't contribute once you enroll in Medicare, so the accumulation window closes around 65 — fund it aggressively before then.
  • Most people leave their HSA in cash — invest it, or you waste its greatest advantage: tax-free growth.

Why the triple advantage is unique. Every other account gives you two of three tax breaks. A traditional 401(k): deduction now and tax-deferred growth, but taxed on the way out. A Roth: tax-free growth and withdrawals, but no deduction going in. The HSA gives you all three at once — a deduction on contributions, tax-free growth, and tax-free withdrawals (for medical costs). Nothing else does. That makes it, dollar for dollar, the most tax-efficient account most people have access to — and the most underused.

Eligibility and 2026 limits. To contribute you must be enrolled in a qualifying HDHP and have no other disqualifying coverage (including Medicare). For 2026 you can contribute: - $4,400 with self-only HDHP coverage, - $8,750 with family coverage, - plus a $1,000 catch-up if you're 55 or older (each spouse's catch-up must go in their own HSA).

Employer contributions count toward these limits. Note a recent expansion: starting January 2026, certain ACA-marketplace bronze and catastrophic plans count as HSA-eligible HDHPs, widening who can contribute.

The stealth-retirement strategy. Here's the move that turns an HSA from a medical checking account into a wealth-builder. Instead of spending HSA dollars on today's doctor visits, pay those small bills out of pocket, leave the HSA invested, and let it grow tax-free for years or decades. Crucially, there's no deadline to reimburse yourself: if you save your receipts, you can withdraw HSA money tax-free years later to reimburse medical expenses you paid long ago. So the HSA doubles as a flexible, tax-free savings account you can tap whenever you want — you just need the paper trail. Practically, this makes a well-funded, invested HSA one of the best retirement accounts you can build.

What changes at 65. At 65 the HSA gets more flexible in two ways. First, the 20% penalty on non-medical withdrawals disappears — after 65 you can withdraw for any purpose and simply pay ordinary income tax, exactly like a traditional IRA. So worst case, your HSA behaves like a traditional IRA; best case (medical use), it's tax-free. Second, you can use HSA funds tax-free to pay Medicare Part B and Part D premiums and other qualified costs (though not Medigap/supplement premiums). Given the healthcare bills ahead in retirement (Chapter 9 — Fidelity estimates $172,500 for a single 65-year-old), a large HSA is a natural, tax-free way to meet them.

The Medicare cutoff. The one hard constraint: once you enroll in Medicare, you can no longer contribute to an HSA. Since most people enroll at 65, your contribution window effectively closes then — and if you delay Medicare while still working, coordinate carefully, because signing up (even for Part A) ends HSA eligibility. The lesson: front-load HSA contributions in the years before 65.

Invest it — don't let it idle. The most common HSA mistake is leaving the balance in cash earning almost nothing, which throws away the tax-free growth that makes the account special. If your HSA provider offers investments (most do above a small cash threshold), invest the balance you won't need for near-term medical costs, the same way you'd invest a Roth IRA for the long term.

Worked example

Letting an HSA compound

At 45, Priya funds her family HSA at $8,750/year, pays her routine medical bills from her checking account, and invests the HSA in a low-cost index fund. Over 20 years at ~7%, her contributions plus growth compound to roughly $370,000 — all of which she can withdraw tax-free for the substantial medical costs of her 60s and 70s, or (after 65) for anything at ordinary rates. Had she spent the HSA on each year's copays instead, she'd have almost nothing at 65.

Key action items

  • Contribute the max while eligible. Fund the HSA fully every year you have HDHP coverage, especially in the decade before 65.
  • Pay current medical bills out of pocket — and save the receipts. Let the HSA grow; reimburse yourself tax-free later.
  • Invest the balance. Don't leave it in cash; treat the long-term portion like a retirement portfolio.
  • Capture the 55+ catch-up. Add the extra $1,000 (in your own HSA; spouses each need their own).
  • Stop contributing before Medicare. Plan your enrollment timing so you don't accidentally over-contribute.
  • Earmark it for retirement healthcare. It's the most tax-efficient way to meet the costs in Chapter 9.
