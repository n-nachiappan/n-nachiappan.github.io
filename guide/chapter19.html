State Taxes on Retirement Income

Summary

  • Federal tax (Chapter 10) is only half the story — your state taxes retirement income too, and the rules vary so much that where you live can swing your tax bill by thousands of dollars a year.
  • Nine states levy no broad income tax at all — Alaska, Florida, Nevada, New Hampshire, South Dakota, Tennessee, Texas, Washington, and Wyoming — so wages, pensions, IRA/401(k) draws, and Social Security are all state-tax-free there.
  • Most states that do have an income tax don't tax Social Security — only eight still do in 2026 (Colorado, Connecticut, Minnesota, Montana, New Mexico, Rhode Island, Utah, Vermont), and most of those exempt lower incomes.
  • The widest variation is in pensions and account withdrawals: a few states exempt them entirely (Illinois, Iowa, Mississippi, Pennsylvania; Michigan as of 2026), many exempt only public or military pensions, and some tax everything.
  • A hidden trap: about 17 states plus D.C. levy their own estate or inheritance tax — often with exemptions far below the federal $15M (Oregon $1M, Massachusetts $2M), and usually no spousal portability (Chapter 26).
  • Federal law protects you when you move — your former state can't tax your pension or retirement-account income once you're a resident elsewhere — but you must genuinely establish domicile, and high-tax states audit departers.

The tax bill you forget about. Everything else in this section is about your federal taxes — brackets, the tax torpedo, IRMAA, RMDs. But most retirees face a second tax system on the same income, and it's wildly inconsistent from state to state. One retiree pays nothing on a $90,000 income; an identical retiree across a state line pays several thousand dollars a year on it. Over a 20- or 30-year retirement, the difference can run into six figures. Because state rules also differ by type of income — Social Security vs. pension vs. IRA withdrawal vs. capital gain — you can't reason about it in the abstract; you have to look up how your specific state treats each stream.

The nine no-income-tax states. Nine states impose no broad personal income tax: Alaska, Florida, Nevada, New Hampshire, South Dakota, Tennessee, Texas, Washington, and Wyoming. In these, every kind of retirement income — Social Security, pensions, IRA and 401(k) distributions, even wages from a part-time job — escapes state income tax entirely. Two footnotes: New Hampshire historically taxed interest and dividends, but that tax has now been repealed; and Washington added a 7% tax on very large capital gains (over ~$262,000 in 2026) that specifically exempts retirement accounts and home sales. The catch is that there's no free lunch — states with no income tax usually lean harder on property and sales taxes (Texas and Washington have notably high property taxes), so judge the total burden, not the headline.

Social Security: taxed federally, mostly spared by states. Up to 85% of your Social Security can be taxable at the federal level (Chapter 10), but the great majority of states leave it alone. Only eight tax any Social Security in 2026 — Colorado, Connecticut, Minnesota, Montana, New Mexico, Rhode Island, Utah, and Vermont — and even those mostly exempt lower- and middle-income retirees through income thresholds. The list has been shrinking (Kansas and West Virginia recently dropped off). For most people, in most states, Social Security is state-tax-free.

Pensions and account withdrawals: where states diverge most. This is the category with the biggest spread. At one end, four states with an income tax fully exempt qualified retirement income: Illinois, Iowa, Mississippi, and Pennsylvania — a retiree living on Social Security, a pension, and IRA withdrawals can owe zero state income tax. Michigan completed a multi-year phase-out in 2026, and now shelters roughly the first $65,000 of retirement income for a single filer (about $131,000 for a couple). Many other states carve out only public or military pensions while fully taxing private pensions and IRA/401(k) draws; still others tax all of it at their ordinary rates. The practical lesson: your state's treatment of a government pension tells you nothing about how it treats your IRA — check each income type separately.

The state estate-tax trap. The federal estate tax is a non-issue for nearly everyone, with its $15 million per-person exemption (Chapter 26). But about 17 states plus the District of Columbia impose their own estate or inheritance tax, and their exemptions can be dramatically lower — Oregon at $1 million (the lowest), Massachusetts at $2 million, Washington around $2.2 million. That's low enough to catch an ordinary house-plus-retirement-accounts estate that the federal tax would never touch. Five states (Kentucky, Maryland, Nebraska, New Jersey, Pennsylvania) tax the heir instead through an inheritance tax; Maryland has both. And unlike the federal system, most of these states offer no spousal portability, so a married couple has to plan deliberately to use both exemptions rather than lose one. If you have a sizable estate, the state you die in can matter far more than the IRS.

Moving for taxes — the right way. Relocation is the biggest lever of all, and federal law makes it work cleanly: under the Source Tax Act of 1996, a state cannot tax the pension or retirement-account income of someone who no longer lives there. So moving from a high-tax state to a no-tax one genuinely escapes your old state's reach on that income — this isn't a loophole, it's the law. But you have to actually change your domicile, not just buy a condo: spend the days (many states use a 183-day test), move your driver's license and voter registration, update your estate documents, and shift the center of your financial and personal life. High-tax states are known to audit departing residents and will look hard at whether you really left. And weigh the whole picture before you uproot: property and sales taxes, cost of living, healthcare access, and — usually most important — proximity to family and the life you want. Don't let the tax tail wag the dog.

Worked example

The state-tax gap

Frank and Rosa have $90,000 of annual retirement income — Social Security, a modest pension, and IRA withdrawals. In a high-rate state they might owe roughly $4,000–$6,000 a year in state income tax; in Florida or Texas, $0 — a gap that compounds past $100,000 over a long retirement. Separately, their $3 million estate (a paid-off home plus retirement accounts) owes nothing in federal estate tax, comfortably under the $15M exemption — but if they retire in Oregon, the state would tax the roughly $2 million above its $1M threshold, a six-figure bill the federal system would never impose. Same couple, same money; the map decides.

Key action items

  • Look up your state, income type by income type. Find how it taxes Social Security, pensions (public vs. private), IRA/401(k) withdrawals, and capital gains — the treatment differs by type.
  • Judge the total tax burden. Weigh income, property, and sales tax together; a “no income tax” state can still be expensive.
  • Factor state tax into where you retire. If you have a pension or large pre-tax accounts, the state difference can be worth six figures over retirement.
  • If you relocate, establish real domicile. Change your license, registration, and the center of your life — and keep records; expect scrutiny from the state you leave.
  • Check for a state estate or inheritance tax. A low state exemption (as little as $1M) can matter far more than the federal one — plan to use both spouses' exemptions.
  • Don't over-optimize. Family, healthcare, and quality of life usually outweigh the tax difference. Let it inform the decision, not dominate it.
