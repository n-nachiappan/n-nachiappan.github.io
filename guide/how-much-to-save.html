How Much to Save — and the Discipline to Do It

Summary

  • The industry rule of thumb is to save at least 15% of gross income for retirement, employer match included, from your mid-20s on — enough, on Fidelity's math, to reach roughly 10× your final salary by age 67.
  • The 10× isn't arbitrary. Retirement costs about 80% of your working income; Social Security covers roughly half of that; the ~45% of final salary left over, divided by a 4.5% withdrawal rate, is 10×. Change any of those three and your own multiple changes with it.
  • If 15% is out of reach today, start where you can and raise the rate 1% a year (or bank half of every raise). The number that matters most is the one you actually sustain.
  • Milestones to check yourself against: about 1× your salary saved by 30, 3× by 40, 6× by 50, 8× by 60, 10× by 67.
  • The engine underneath the rate is one habit: spend less than you earn, and automate the gap so saving happens before you can spend it. A frame like 50/30/20 — needs, wants, saving-and-debt — makes it concrete.
  • High-interest debt is a negative investment. Paying off a 22% credit card is a guaranteed, tax-free 22% return — better than any fund. Clear it before you invest beyond the match.
  • Hold a 3–6 month emergency fund so a job loss or car repair doesn't force you to sell investments or reach for a card.
  • Starting early beats saving more later. Because of compounding, a saver who invests for ten years in her 20s and then stops can out-finish someone who saves three times as much but starts a decade later.
  • Your savings rate is a purchase order for time. The FIRE movement's 50% rate reaches independence in ~17 years — but it's the same equation that turns 15% into "retire at 67." You needn't adopt the lifestyle to use the arithmetic, and part-time work is the cheapest lever there is: every $10,000 of it replaces $250,000 of portfolio.

The one number to anchor on: 15%. Almost every large plan provider converges on the same target — save at least 15% of your gross (pre-tax) income every year, and count the employer match toward it. Fidelity's widely-cited framework assumes a saver who does this steadily from age 25 to 67 will accumulate about 10 times their final working salary — roughly what it takes, alongside Social Security, to replace about 80% of pre-retirement income for a 30-year retirement. (Why 80%, and why 10×? The first worked example below does the arithmetic.) If your employer matches 5%, you only have to find 10% yourself to hit the mark. That match is the single best deal in personal finance, and Chapter 1 covers why to capture all of it first.

What if 15% is impossible right now? For many people early on — student loans, entry-level pay, a first mortgage — 15% is genuinely out of reach, and that's fine. The rule that beats every other rule is: start, and escalate. Save whatever you can today, capture the full match no matter what, and then raise your contribution rate by one percentage point each year — most 401(k) plans have an "auto-increase" toggle that does this for you painlessly. Bank half of every raise before your spending rises to meet it. A saver who starts at 6% and climbs 1% a year lands at 15% in nine years without ever feeling a cut in take-home pay.

Check yourself against the milestones. Rates are the input; balances are the output. Fidelity's savings milestones give you a rough yardstick to know whether you're on track, expressed as multiples of your current salary:

These are guideposts, not verdicts. They assume you'll lean on Social Security for part of your income and retire around 67; retire earlier, spend more, or expect a smaller benefit, and your personal target rises. Behind them all: savings are expected to replace only about 45% of your pre-retirement income — Social Security is assumed to cover the rest of what you need — which is why how benefits are earned and when you claim them matter as much as your savings rate.

Worked example

Where the 10× comes from

The multiple isn't handed down from anywhere — it's three assumptions multiplied together. Take a saver whose final salary at 67 is $100,000.

The lesson: 10× is a conditional number, and now you can see its conditions. It assumes you retire at 67, claim Social Security at 67, earn near the median, and are content with 80% of your working income. Each assumption is a dial. Retire at 60 and you lose seven years of contributions, add seven years of spending, and get a smaller benefit — the multiple climbs well past 10×. Earn $300k and Social Security replaces far less of your income (the benefit formula is deliberately progressive), so your multiple rises too. Run your numbers through these five rows rather than adopting someone else's answer.

The habit under the number: spend less than you earn. Every savings rate is really a spending decision. You cannot save 15% if you spend 100%. The durable move is to make saving the first bill you pay, not the leftover — "pay yourself first" — and automate it so the money is swept into the 401(k) and investment accounts before it ever hits your checking balance. What you don't see, you don't spend. A popular scaffold is the 50/30/20 rule: of your after-tax income, roughly 50% goes to needs (housing, food, utilities, minimum debt payments), 30% to wants (dining out, travel, subscriptions), and 20% to saving and extra debt paydown. The percentages aren't sacred — in a high-cost city "needs" may crowd out "wants" — but the discipline of naming every dollar's job is the point.

Wants and needs are a muscle, not a moral test. The goal isn't to never enjoy money; it's to notice the difference between a need and a want before you spend, and to keep lifestyle creep from eating every raise. The biggest levers are the big three — housing, transportation, and food — not the daily coffee. Buying slightly less house and driving a car a few years longer frees up more savings capacity than any amount of small-scale frugality, and does it without the willpower tax of tracking every latte.

Kill high-interest debt before you invest beyond the match. Investing is uncertain; debt is not. Paying off a credit card charging 22% is a guaranteed, tax-free 22% return — an outcome no investment can promise. So the order of operations is: (1) capture the full employer match — that's an instant 50–100% return you never turn down; (2) build a starter emergency cushion; (3) attack high-interest debt hard; then (4) invest in earnest. To pay debt down, the avalanche method (highest interest rate first) saves the most money; the snowball method (smallest balance first) gives faster psychological wins and better follow-through. The best method is the one you'll actually finish. Low-rate debt — a sub-6% mortgage, a 0% car loan — is a different question entirely, and gets its own chapter next.

Hold an emergency fund so life doesn't derail the plan. Keep three to six months of essential expenses in cash or a high-yield savings account — more if your income is lumpy or your job is less secure. This is not an investment; it's insurance against having to sell stocks in a downturn or run up a card when the transmission fails. Without it, the first emergency undoes years of discipline. With it, market crashes and surprise bills become inconveniences rather than catastrophes.

Why the early years matter most: compounding. The dollars you save in your 20s and 30s are worth far more at retirement than the dollars you save in your 50s, because they have decades longer to compound. This is the quiet, counterintuitive heart of the whole enterprise: time in the market does more heavy lifting than the size of your contributions. A modest amount saved early, then left alone, routinely beats a larger amount saved late. F4 shows what a century of returns does to patient money. The two examples below make the point from opposite directions: the first shows how much of a lifetime pot compounding builds rather than you, and the second shows what a single decade of delay costs.

Worked example

How 15% a year turns into 10× — and who does the work

Maya starts at 25 on a $60,000 salary, saves 15% including her match, never changes the rate, and retires at 67. Her salary grows 4% a year and her portfolio returns 6% a year. (Inflation cancels out of a multiple — both her salary and her balance inflate together — so what drives the answer is the 2-point gap between the 6% return and the 4% raises.)

At ageSalaryPut in so farGrowth so farBalance× salary

Read the two middle columns against each other — that's the whole compounding story in one table. Maya puts in $943,000 over 42 years and retires with $3.04 million. She personally supplied under a third of it; 69% of her pot is growth she never earned at work. The crossover comes at about age 49 — that's when accumulated growth passes everything she has ever contributed, and her portfolio starts out-earning her savings habit. Her last ten years alone add $1.63 million to the balance while only $379,000 of that is new money.

The lesson: the 15% rate is doing less of the work than the 42 years are. That's also why the balance looks so discouraging early — at 30 Maya has $58,000 and it feels like nothing, because compounding pays out at the end of a long runway, not the beginning. The reader who quits at 32 because the balance is unimpressive is quitting right before the part that matters. Two honest caveats: this path runs slightly under the milestones earlier in this chapter in the middle years (0.8× at 30, not 1×), which is a fair reminder that they're guideposts rather than verdicts; and the multiple is sensitive to the return you assume — nudge 6% to 7% and the same saver lands near 12× instead of 9.7×. Treat 10× as a central estimate, not a law.

Worked example

The ten-year head start that never gets caught

Two savers, same 7% average return, same $6,000 a year — the only difference is when they start and stop.

SaverContributesYearsTotal put inBalance at 67
Ellie (early)Ages 25–34, then stops10$60,000~$770,000

Ellie saves for just ten years in her 20s and never adds another dollar. Liam starts at 35 and saves diligently for thirty-two straight years. Liam contributes more than three times as much money — and still retires with less.

The lesson: Ellie's edge isn't discipline or income — it's a decade of extra compounding on her earliest dollars. You can't buy that decade back later at any contribution rate. If you're young, the single most valuable thing you can do is start now, even small. If you're not young, the second-most valuable thing is to start today rather than next year — and lean harder on the catch-up contributions age 50+ unlocks.

Turning the dial: the FIRE movement

What happens if you don't stop at 15%. Everything above assumes you accept the default deal: save a modest slice, work about forty years, retire around 67. A movement known as FIRE — Financial Independence, Retire Early — asks what happens if you refuse it. Its recipe is blunt: save 50% or more of your take-home pay and retire on 25× your annual expenses, which is simply the 4% rule turned upside down. The ideas trace to Vicki Robin and Joe Dominguez's Your Money or Your Life (1992), were sharpened by Jacob Lund Fisker's Early Retirement Extreme (2010), and reached a wide audience through Pete Adeney's Mr. Money Mustache blog — especially his 2012 post "The Shockingly Simple Math Behind Early Retirement" — before breaking into the mainstream press around 2018.

The insight worth stealing, even if you never retire early: your savings rate is the whole story. Not your income, and not — within reason — your returns. The reason is structural, and once seen it can't be unseen: your savings rate sets both sides of the equation at once. Saving half your take-home pay means you're piling up money twice as fast and that the life you must eventually fund costs half as much. Income largely cancels out. Someone on $60,000 saving half reaches independence at the same time as someone on $600,000 saving half — the second person just does it in a bigger house. Run the standard assumptions (5% real returns, starting from zero, 25× expenses) and the whole plan collapses to one column:

Look closely at the 15% row. It says 43 years — start at 25 and you are done at 68. That is the mainstream advice this chapter opened with, and it is not a coincidence. FIRE isn't a rival philosophy with different math; it's the same equation with the dial moved. "Retire at 67" is not a law of nature, it's the output of choosing 15%. That reframing is the movement's real contribution, and it's useful even to a reader with no intention of retiring at 40: every extra point of savings rate buys back time, and the table tells you exactly how much.

Worked example

Sam, at 50% — and the three doors it opens

Sam is 25, takes home $90,000, and lives on $45,000 — saving the other $45,000, a 50% rate. Assume 5% real returns. Sam's full-independence number is 25 × $45,000 = $1,125,000. But "quit forever" is only one of the doors that a growing pot opens, and it's the slowest one:

DoorWhat it means in practicePot neededSam gets there at
Coast FIREStop saving entirely. Never touch the pot — just earn enough to cover this year's $45,000. What's already invested compounds untouched to $1.125M by 65.$194,300age 29

Full FIRE (28.6×)Same, but drawing a safer 3.5% — see the caveat below.$1,285,700age 43

The Coast number is the one that should stop you. After just four years of saving hard, Sam has $194,300 — and if he never contributes another dollar, that alone compounds for 36 years into the full $1.125M at 65. He isn't retired at 29. He's something arguably better: free. He can take the interesting job that pays $45,000, or go part-time, or start something risky, and his retirement is already handled. Note the ordering, too — Barista FIRE arrives at 36, a full six years before Full FIRE at 42, because part-time work does double duty: it shrinks the pot you need and keeps the pot growing while you decide.

The lesson: the movement's name oversells its own product. The valuable thing here isn't the "RE" — it's the FI, and it shows up decades before the finish line. Most of what people actually want from early retirement (say no to bad work, take the pay cut, stop being afraid of a layoff) is bought at the first row of this table, not the last. Sam's assumptions are also doing real work: a 50% rate on $90,000 take-home is far easier than on $45,000, where the same percentage means living on $22,500. Income doesn't change the math, but it very much changes whether the math is livable.

The vocabulary, and a warning about it. The variants above come with community-coined labels — Lean FIRE (a deliberately frugal target, often under $40,000 a year), Fat FIRE (a full-fat lifestyle, often $100,000+), Coast FIRE, and Barista FIRE (widely said to be named for part-time jobs at employers like Starbucks that extend health benefits to part-time staff — a story repeated everywhere and sourced nowhere, so treat the etymology as folklore). These are folk taxonomy, not defined terms: no regulator, academic, or standards body defines them, and popular write-ups routinely blur the two that matter most. Keep them straight: Coast means you are neither contributing nor withdrawing; Barista means you are withdrawing, just less, because work covers the rest.

Part-time work is the most underrated lever in the whole exercise. Because the 4% rule only applies to the share of spending your portfolio funds, any outside income reduces the required pot linearly — and the multiplier is startling. Every $10,000 of annual part-time income replaces $250,000 of portfolio (at a 4% draw; about $286,000 at 3.5%). A two-day-a-week consulting gig at $25,000 is worth $625,000 you don't have to accumulate. And it pays a second dividend that rarely gets counted: earning something in the first decade of retirement blunts sequence-of-returns risk precisely when the portfolio is most vulnerable to it, because every dollar you earn is a dollar you don't have to sell into a bad market. It can also carry health coverage and add Social Security earnings years — the two problems below.

Where the math gets genuinely contested: the 4% rule was never built for a 50-year retirement. This is FIRE's weakest joint, and it deserves a straight answer rather than a reassuring one. Bengen's 1994 work and the 1998 Trinity study tested 30-year horizons — Trinity never modeled beyond 30 years at all. Retiring at 42 asks the portfolio to last fifty-plus, a case the foundational research simply never examined. What replaces 4% is a live disagreement, and the two most credible answers don't match:

Read those last rows against each other. Michael Kitces, working from historical returns, finds the penalty for a long horizon flattens — stretching from 30 to 45 years costs only half a point, and he suggests "the classic 25X post-retirement spending target… should perhaps be a 28X rule instead (or 30X for those who prefer to be a little more conservative)." Morningstar, using forward-looking returns that reflect today's equity valuations, is already at 3.3% at forty years — below Kitces at forty-five — and its curve is still falling. So "3.25–3.5%" is the historical-method answer, not a settled consensus. The honest summary: a FIRE target is more like 28–31× expenses than 25×, and the difference is a few more years of work. Worth knowing too: Morningstar's own base case has swung between 3.3% and 4.0% across five annual reports, which is a good reminder that these are estimates that move, not constants to engineer your life around.

Three practical obstacles the spreadsheets tend to skip. First and largest: health insurance. Medicare starts at 65, so retiring at 42 means buying your own coverage for twenty-three years — and the ground shifted underneath this recently. The ACA's enhanced subsidies expired on December 31, 2025, which brought back the hard 400%-of-poverty subsidy cliff: for 2026, a single filer one dollar over $62,600 loses the entire credit, and an unsubsidized benchmark Silver plan for a 60-year-old averages roughly $15,900 a year. The saving grace is that subsidies key off MAGI, not wealth, and a FIRE household living on Roth withdrawals and taxable basis can often show a low MAGI on a large portfolio — but it is now a knife-edge rather than a gentle slope. Chapter 26 has the mechanics. Second: your money is locked up. Retire at 42 and the 10% early-withdrawal penalty stands between you and most of your pot until 59½ — which is what Rule 72(t)/SEPP and the Rule of 55 exist to solve, and why FIRE savers deliberately overweight taxable and Roth accounts to build a bridge. Third: Social Security shrinks — but less than you fear. Your benefit averages your highest 35 years, so retiring at 42 stuffs the average with zeros. The panic-inducing version of this ("I worked 17 of 35 years, so I lose half my benefit") is simply wrong: the PIA formula is steeply progressive, replacing the first $1,286 of monthly indexed earnings at 90% but earnings above $7,749 at just 15%. The zeros knock out your marginal years, credited at 15–32 cents on the dollar — so the loss is real, but far smaller than proportional. (You do need 40 credits — about ten working years — to qualify at all.)

So what should a normal reader take from this? Probably not 50%. The median U.S. household income is around $84,000, and a 50% rate is a fantasy for most families with children, rent, and student loans — the movement's most-cited poster cases tend to be software engineers on six figures, and it's worth being clear-eyed that income buys the option to save half even if it doesn't change the arithmetic. Nor is the deterministic 5%-forever table above a promise: run the same savings rates against real historical return sequences and the same 50% saver arrives anywhere between roughly 6 and 19 years, depending on the market they happened to get. But the reframing survives all of that intact. Your savings rate is a purchase order for time. You don't have to buy 25 years of it. Going from 15% to 20% pulls your finish line in by roughly six years — and Coast FIRE says that even a few early years of aggressive saving can buy the freedom to stop worrying about it forever. That is the same lesson as Ellie's ten-year head start, priced in years instead of dollars.

Myths & facts

Myth"I don't earn enough to invest. I'll start once I'm making real money."
FactThe amount matters far less than the start date. $50 a month at 25 outruns $150 a month begun at 40, because the early dollars compound for an extra 15 years. Waiting for a bigger salary usually means waiting forever — expenses rise to meet income. Start with what you have and escalate.
Myth"Budgeting means giving up everything fun."
FactA budget isn't a cage; it's permission to spend the 30% guilt-free once the 20% is saved. The point is to automate the saving first so what's left is genuinely yours. Most of the real savings come from the big three — housing, cars, food — not from policing small joys.
Myth"I should invest my spare cash even while carrying a credit-card balance — the market returns more."
FactThe market might return ~7%; the card will charge ~22%. Paying off high-interest debt is a guaranteed, tax-free return no investment can match. Grab the employer match, then clear the card, then invest.
Myth"A raise means I can finally afford a nicer lifestyle."
FactLetting spending rise to match every raise — lifestyle creep — is why high earners still reach 50 with little saved. Bank at least half of each raise before you feel it, and your savings rate climbs without any sense of sacrifice.
Myth"FIRE is for tech bros on $400k. There's nothing in it for me."
FactThe lifestyle may be out of reach — a 50% savings rate genuinely is, for most households — but the insight is free. Your savings rate is a purchase order for time, and the table works at every rate: moving from 15% to 20% pulls your finish line in about six years. You don't have to take the whole program to use the arithmetic.
Myth"Retiring early would wreck my Social Security — I'd have half the earnings years."
FactIt shrinks the benefit, but nothing like proportionally. Benefits average your best 35 years, so early retirement does add zeros — but the formula replaces your first $1,286/month of indexed earnings at 90% and everything over $7,749 at only 15%. The zeros displace your marginal years, not your base. You do need 40 credits (~10 years) to qualify at all.
Myth"Financial independence means never working again."
FactThat's the slowest and most expensive version of it. Coast FIRE (stop saving, let the pot ride) and Barista FIRE (part-time work covers part of the bill) arrive years earlier — and every $10,000 of part-time income replaces $250,000 you'd otherwise have to accumulate. Most of what people want from early retirement is available long before "never work again" is.

Key action items

  • Target 15% of gross income, match included. Can't get there yet? Save what you can, capture the full match no matter what, and turn on auto-escalation to add 1% a year.
  • Automate everything. Route contributions straight from payroll so saving happens before the money reaches checking. Pay yourself first.
  • Name every dollar. Use 50/30/20 or any frame that separates needs, wants, and saving — and aim your cuts at housing, transport, and food, not the small stuff.
  • Follow the order of operations. Match → starter emergency fund → high-interest debt → invest in earnest. Don't skip step three.
  • Build a 3–6 month emergency fund in a high-yield savings account so a shock never forces you to sell investments.
  • Start today. The most expensive year is the one you spend waiting to begin. Check yourself against the 1×/3×/6×/8×/10× milestones and course-correct early.
  • Price your savings rate in years, not dollars. Find your rate on the FIRE table and see what one more point buys. Then work out your Coast number — the balance that, left untouched, compounds to your target by 65 — because it's usually far closer than you think, and hitting it means you never have to save again.
