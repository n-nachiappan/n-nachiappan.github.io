Sequence-of-Returns Risk and How to Blunt It

Summary

  • Sequence-of-returns risk is the danger that poor returns early in retirement — while you're also withdrawing — do permanent damage, even if your long-run average return is fine.
  • Two retirees with the identical average return over 30 years can end up rich or broke depending on the order of good and bad years.
  • Order only matters because you withdraw. With no money going in or out, the sequence is mathematically irrelevant — the same growth factors multiply to the same answer in any order. It is the withdrawal from a fallen portfolio that turns a paper loss into a permanent one, which is why every real mitigation is a way to sell less at the bottom.
  • The risk is concentrated in the first 5–10 years of retirement (the "fragile decade"). A crash then is far worse than the same crash later.
  • Core mitigations: a cash/bond buffer so you don't sell stocks into a downturn, spending flexibility (cutting in bad years), guardrails, delaying retirement or Social Security, and a sensible, diversified allocation.
  • Guyton-Klinger guardrails allow higher starting withdrawals by adjusting spending up or down within bands — but critics (Kitces) note they can demand painful 30%+ cuts in a truly bad sequence.
  • Newer risk-based guardrails aim to require much smaller adjustments.
  • A further mitigation is to borrow rather than sell in a bad year — a credit line breaks the forced-sale link outright, and loan proceeds aren't taxable income. It only helps if the loan is small and can't be called at the bottom; Chapter 18 covers the instruments, the sizing, and who shouldn't use them.
  • The cheapest, most reliable defense is willingness to trim spending temporarily when markets fall early.

The mechanism. In the accumulation phase, a market crash is almost a gift — you keep buying shares cheap and recover. In the decumulation phase, it's the opposite. When you sell shares to fund spending during a downturn, you lock in losses and sell more shares to raise the same dollars, permanently shrinking the base that later good years compound on. Even if the market eventually delivers a great average return, an early hole you sold into may never refill. That's sequence risk: same average, different order, different outcome.

Why order matters at all — and it isn't the market. It is worth being precise here, because the precision points straight at the fix. If you neither add nor withdraw a dollar, the order of your returns is irrelevant: your ending balance is your starting balance multiplied by every year's growth factor, and multiplication doesn't care what order you do it in. Sequence risk is therefore not a property of markets at all — it is a property of cash flows meeting markets. The withdrawal is what breaks the symmetry: selling shares at a depressed price permanently removes the shares that would have carried the recovery, so a dollar taken out at the bottom costs you far more than the same dollar taken out at the top. That is the entire mechanism, and it has a useful corollary — anything that shrinks, postpones, or replaces the withdrawal in bad years attacks the risk at its root. Every mitigation below is a version of that one idea.

Worked example

Same five returns, opposite orders

Two retirees each start with $1,000,000 and get exactly the same five annual returns — +25%, +18%, +12%, −15%, −25% — one in that order, the other in reverse. If neither withdraws anything, both end at exactly $1,053,150. The ordering is worth nothing at all. Now have each withdraw $50,000 at the end of every year:

Identical returns, identical average, identical everything but the order. The $141,859 gap didn't come from the market — it came from the $50,000 a year, and specifically from the two years in which that money was raised by selling into a fall. Stretch the same effect across a thirty-year retirement and it is the difference between money that lasts and money that doesn't.

The fragile decade. Research consistently finds the danger is front-loaded: returns in roughly the first 5–10 years of retirement do the most to determine success or failure. A retiree who hits a bad market at 66 is in real jeopardy; the same market at 86 is a footnote. This is why the years right around your retirement date deserve the most caution — and why the classic 4% rule, which ignores order entirely, can still fail an unlucky 1968-style retiree.

Mitigation 1 — a cash/bond buffer (the bucket approach). Hold one to three years of spending in cash and short-term bonds. When stocks fall, spend from the buffer instead of selling equities into the decline, giving the stock portion time to recover. Refill the buffer in good years. This "bucket strategy" doesn't raise your long-run return, but it directly attacks the sequence-risk mechanism by removing the need to sell low.

Mitigation 2 — spending flexibility. The single most powerful and cheapest defense is a willingness to spend less temporarily when markets drop early. Even modest, temporary cuts in the first few bad years dramatically improve survival, because you preserve shares for the recovery. A retiree who can trim discretionary spending 10–15% in a downturn is vastly safer than one locked into a fixed real withdrawal.

Mitigation 3 — guardrails (and their limits). Guardrail systems formalize flexibility. The best-known, Guyton-Klinger, sets bands (commonly ±20%) around your withdrawal rate and cuts spending ~10% when you drift too high (or raises it when you're well below), which lets you start at a higher rate (5%+) because you'll adjust as you go. The catch, as Michael Kitces has pointed out, is that in a genuinely bad sequence real-world Guyton-Klinger can force cumulative cuts of 30% or more — a brutal lifestyle hit for someone already retired. Newer risk-based guardrails (Fitzpatrick/Tharp) trigger off your probability of success rather than fixed bands and tend to require far smaller adjustments — on the order of a few percent — for the same protection. The lesson: guardrails help, but know how deep the cuts can get in the worst case before you rely on a high starting rate.

Mitigation 4 — delay or work a little longer. Retiring into an expensive market is riskier; a short delay, part-time income, or delaying Social Security (Chapter 14) all reduce the portfolio's early burden. Even one or two years of continued earning shrinks the fragile decade's exposure.

Mitigation 5 — sensible allocation. Neither too-aggressive (deep early drawdowns) nor too-conservative (can't outrun inflation over 30 years). A diversified stock/bond mix with a modest cash reserve is the workhorse. Some advisors add a "rising equity glidepath" — starting retirement a bit more conservative and drifting back into stocks — precisely to reduce fragile-decade exposure.

Mitigation 6 — borrow instead of selling. A cash buffer works by having already sold; a line of credit works by letting you postpone selling altogether. Draw on a pledged-asset line, a HELOC, or a HECM standby line (Chapter 17) to fund a bad year, then repay out of the recovery — the shares stay invested and the loss stays on paper. Because loan proceeds aren't income, the move also sidesteps the capital gain, the extra Social Security taxation, and the IRMAA exposure that an equivalent withdrawal would have triggered. Two cautions decide whether it helps or hurts: most portfolio lines carry variable rates — roughly 6–8% in 2026 — and most are demand loans a lender can call at the bottom, the precise moment the strategy exists to survive. Opened in advance and kept small, it is a genuine buffer; drawn heavily, it amplifies sequence risk instead of hedging it. Chapter 18 works through the instruments, the sizing, and who should stay away.

Key action items

  • Respect the fragile decade. Be most cautious in the 5–10 years around your retirement date.
  • Hold a buffer. Keep 1–3 years of spending in cash/short bonds so you never sell stocks into a crash.
  • Pre-commit to flexibility. Decide now what you'll cut, and by how much, if the first few years go badly.
  • If you use guardrails, know the downside. Understand how deep Guyton-Klinger cuts can go; consider risk-based guardrails for gentler adjustments.
  • Consider delaying. A short work extension or delayed Social Security meaningfully lowers early risk.
  • Line up credit before you need it. A pledged-asset or HECM standby line arranged in a calm market gives you a way to fund a bad year without selling — see Chapter 18 for how to size it so it can't be called against you.
  • Diversify and avoid extremes. Keep an allocation that can survive an early crash and outrun 30 years of inflation.
