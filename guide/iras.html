IRAs: Traditional and Spousal

Summary

  • An IRA (Individual Retirement Arrangement) is a retirement account you open yourself, independent of any employer — you control the provider, the investments, and the fees.
  • For 2026 you can contribute up to $7,500 across all your IRAs, plus a $1,100 catch-up at 50+.
  • You need earned income at least equal to what you contribute.
  • A traditional IRA may be tax-deductible now — but if you (or your spouse) are covered by a workplace plan, the deduction phases out at higher incomes, above which you can still contribute non-deductibly (which sets up the backdoor Roth in Chapter 3).
  • The spousal IRA is the key rule most couples miss.
  • A spouse with little or no income can still fund a full IRA, using the working spouse's earned income, as long as you file jointly.
  • Compared with a 401(k), an IRA has a lower contribution limit but far more freedom — any brokerage, any fund, usually lower costs.
  • IRAs are also where old 401(k)s often land after you change jobs (a "rollover IRA").
  • Self-employed or running a small business? The SEP-IRA and SIMPLE IRA are IRA-based plans with far higher limits than a personal IRA — a SEP shelters up to $72,000 in 2026.

What an IRA is. Unlike a 401(k), which your employer sponsors, an IRA is opened by you at a bank or brokerage. That independence is its advantage: you're not stuck with a limited menu of funds or a plan's fee structure — you can hold nearly any stock, bond, ETF, or mutual fund, and shop for the lowest costs. The tradeoff is a smaller annual limit.

The 2026 limits and the earned-income rule. You can contribute up to $7,500 total across all your IRAs (traditional and Roth combined), plus $1,100 more if you're 50 or older — so $8,600 for older savers. One firm requirement: you must have earned income (wages or self-employment income) at least equal to your contribution. Investment income, Social Security, and pensions don't count. This is where the spousal rule becomes essential.

Traditional IRA deductibility. Contributions to a traditional IRA may be deductible, but the rules depend on whether you're covered by a workplace plan: - If neither you nor your spouse is covered by a workplace plan, your traditional IRA contribution is fully deductible regardless of income. - If you are covered by a workplace plan, the deduction phases out for 2026 between $81,000–$91,000 of income (single) and $129,000–$149,000 (married filing jointly). - If your spouse is covered but you aren't, your deduction phases out between $242,000–$252,000 (joint).

Above these ranges you can still make a traditional IRA contribution — it's just non-deductible. Non-deductible contributions create "basis" you track on Form 8606 so you're not taxed twice later, and they're the raw material for the backdoor Roth (Chapter 3).

The spousal IRA — a full account for the non-earner. Normally you need your own earned income to fund an IRA. The spousal IRA is the exception, and it's genuinely valuable: if you file jointly and one spouse has enough earned income, the other spouse can fund a full IRA of their own — up to the same $7,500 (+$1,100 catch-up) — even with zero personal income. The contribution goes into an account in the non-earning spouse's own name (it's still individual). The only limit is that the working spouse's earned income must cover both contributions combined.

Worked example

Doubling up with a spousal IRA

Maria earns $120,000; her husband David is a stay-at-home parent with no wages. Without the spousal rule, David couldn't fund an IRA at all. With it, and because they file jointly, David opens his own IRA and contributes the full $7,500 (funded from household income). The couple now shelters $15,000 across two IRAs instead of $7,500 — doubling their IRA savings and building retirement assets in both names, which matters for tax diversification and for the survivor down the road.

Traditional IRA vs. 401(k) — when each shines. They're complements, not rivals. The 401(k) has a much higher limit ($24,500 vs. $7,500) and often an employer match, so it's usually the first priority up to the match. The IRA's edge is choice and cost — no plan menu, no plan fees. A common sequence: fund the 401(k) to the match, then max an IRA (often a Roth, Chapter 3), then return to the 401(k) for more.

The rollover IRA. When you leave a job, rolling an old 401(k) into an IRA is common — it consolidates accounts and usually cuts fees. Just remember the two cautions from elsewhere in this book: rolling to an IRA can forfeit the Rule of 55 (Chapter 24), and a large pre-tax IRA balance can complicate a future backdoor Roth via the pro-rata rule (Chapter 3). Roll with intent, not by reflex.

For the self-employed: the SEP-IRA and SIMPLE IRA

Two IRA-based plans for business owners. Beyond the personal IRA above, the tax code offers two employer-style IRAs for the self-employed and small businesses. They round out the menu alongside the Solo 401(k) from Chapter 1 once you have self-employment income.

The SEP-IRA — simple and high-capacity. A Simplified Employee Pension is the easiest big-contribution plan to run. It's funded entirely by the employer (you), with a contribution of up to 25% of compensation — about 20% of net self-employment income for a sole proprietor — capped at $72,000 in 2026. There's almost no paperwork: no annual Form 5500, and you can open and fund it as late as your tax-filing deadline, extensions included — which makes it a popular last-minute move at tax time. The tradeoffs: there's no employee deferral and no catch-up, so at a given income it shelters less than a Solo 401(k); and if you have employees, you must contribute the same percentage of pay for every eligible one, which gets expensive fast — so the SEP shines mainly for solo operators. (Roth SEPs are newly permitted under SECURE 2.0, though not every provider offers them yet.)

The SIMPLE IRA — for a small business with staff. A Savings Incentive Match Plan for Employees suits a small business (up to 100 employees) that wants to let workers defer their own pay. Employees can defer up to $17,000 in 2026, plus a $4,000 catch-up at 50+ (or $5,250 at 60–63), and the employer must either match up to 3% of pay or contribute a flat 2% for everyone. The limits sit below the SEP and Solo 401(k), and one trap stands out: money withdrawn within the first two years of participating faces a 25% early-withdrawal penalty — steeper than the usual 10%.

Which one? For a solo earner chasing the biggest deduction, the Solo 401(k) usually wins — its employee deferral shelters more, and it adds a Roth option. The SEP-IRA is the pick when you value zero paperwork over the last few thousand dollars of contribution room. The SIMPLE IRA fits a genuine small business with a handful of employees.

Key action items

  • Open and fund an IRA — for both spouses. If you're married and file jointly, use the spousal IRA so a non-earning spouse gets a full account too.
  • Check your deduction eligibility. If a workplace plan covers you, see where your income falls in the phase-out before assuming the contribution is deductible.
  • Track non-deductible basis on Form 8606. If you contribute above the deduction limits, file it every year so you're not taxed twice.
  • Sequence with your 401(k). Match first, then IRA, then more 401(k) — adjust for your fee and fund options.
  • Consolidate old 401(k)s thoughtfully. A rollover IRA simplifies life, but weigh the Rule-of-55 and pro-rata implications first.
  • Have self-employment income? Add a SEP or SIMPLE. A SEP-IRA shelters up to $72,000 in 2026 with almost no paperwork — but compare it against the Solo 401(k) (Chapter 1) first.
