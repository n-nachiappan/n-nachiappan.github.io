Reference · Appendix C

Federal Tax Tables (2026)

The idea

  • The bracket tables the rest of the guide keeps pointing at — the ordinary-income rates that hit 401(k)/IRA withdrawals, pensions, and taxable Social Security, and the separate, gentler long-term capital-gains rates — for all four filing statuses, at 2026 figures.
  • Brackets are marginal: a rate applies only to the dollars inside its band, not to your whole income. Your effective rate is always lower than your top bracket.
  • Every rate reads off taxable income — what's left after your standard deduction. Seniors get an unusually large deduction in 2026, so the first chunk of income is taxed at 0%.
  • This appendix is the detail behind Chapter 10 (tax optimization) and Chapter 6 (the 0% gains bracket); pair it with Appendix B for the non-bracket figures.

Two rate schedules run in parallel, and keeping them straight is the heart of retirement tax planning:

Worked example

Why your top bracket isn't your tax rate

A single retiree has $60,000 of taxable income. Being "in the 22% bracket" does not mean a $13,200 tax bill — each band is taxed at its own rate:

BandTaxed atTax
First $12,40010%$1,240
$12,400 – $50,400 ($38,000)12%$4,560
$50,400 – $60,000 ($9,600)22%$2,112
Total federal income tax$7,912

That's a 13.2% effective rate even though the marginal rate — what the next dollar withdrawn would cost — is 22%. Tax planning runs on the marginal rate (it prices your next Roth conversion or withdrawal); your budget runs on the effective rate.

3 · Extra deductions for age 65+ (and the blind)

This is where retirees pull ahead. Three separate add-ons stack on top of the base standard deduction:

Add-on2026 amountWho qualifies
Age / blindness additional standard deduction$2,050 (single / HoH)
$1,650 (per married spouse)		Each condition counts separately — a single filer who is both 65+ and blind gets it twice; a married 65+ spouse who is also blind gets $1,650 × 2.
Senior bonus deduction (temporary, tax years 2025–2028)$6,000 per person 65+New under the 2025 One Big Beautiful Bill Act. Phases out above $75,000 MAGI (single) / $150,000 (joint), at 6% of the excess, and disappears entirely by ~$175k / ~$250k. Available whether you itemize or not.
Worked example

How much a 65+ retiree shields before owing a dime

Stack the base deduction, the age add-on, and the temporary bonus:

ComponentSingle, 65+MFJ, both 65+
Base standard deduction$16,100$32,200
Age 65+ additional$2,050$3,300
Senior bonus (through 2028)$6,000$12,000
Income shielded at 0%$24,150$47,500

A single 65-year-old can pull roughly $24,150, and a couple both 65+ about $47,500, of ordinary income before any federal income tax applies — then the 10% and 12% bands stretch well beyond that. For many middle-income retirees this zeroes out federal tax on a large slice of income. It's a limited-time window: plan Roth conversions and bracket-filling around it while the $6,000 bonus lasts (2025–2028).

4 · Long-term capital-gains & qualified-dividend rates

Assets held longer than a year, and qualified dividends, ride this separate, gentler schedule. Crucially, gains stack on top of your ordinary income — it's your total taxable income that decides which gains rate applies. Keep ordinary income low in a given year and you can realize gains at 0%.

Thresholds are measured on total taxable income (ordinary income plus the gains themselves). A separate 3.8% Net Investment Income Tax applies to investment income once modified AGI exceeds $200,000 (single) / $250,000 (joint) — those two thresholds are fixed in law and not indexed to inflation. See Chapter 6 and Chapter 10 for how to harvest gains inside the 0% band.

5 · When Social Security itself becomes taxable

Not a bracket, but a threshold table the guide leans on constantly: how much of your Social Security benefit is pulled into ordinary income depends on your "combined income" (AGI + tax-exempt interest + ½ of your benefits). Unlike almost everything else here, these thresholds are fixed in law and have never been indexed to inflation — so more retirees cross them every year (the "tax torpedo," Chapter 10).

Share of benefits that becomes taxableSingle / HoHMarried filing jointly
0% taxableunder $25,000under $32,000
up to 50% taxable$25,000 – $34,000$32,000 – $44,000
up to 85% taxableover $34,000over $44,000

"Up to 85%" is a ceiling on the share of benefits taxed, not a tax rate — the included amount is then taxed at your ordinary rate from the tables above. Married filing separately (if you lived with your spouse) generally has a $0 threshold, taxing benefits almost immediately.

Figures are the 2026 inflation-adjusted federal amounts (IRS Rev. Proc. 2025-32, with senior-deduction rules from the 2025 One Big Beautiful Bill Act). Brackets and deductions reset annually with inflation; the Social Security and Net Investment Income Tax thresholds do not. State income tax is separate — see Chapter 19. Nothing here is personalized financial, tax, or legal advice; confirm current-year figures and your own situation with a CPA or the IRS before acting.
