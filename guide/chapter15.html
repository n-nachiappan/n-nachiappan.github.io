Annuities: Buying Your Own Guaranteed Income

Summary

  • An annuity is a contract with an insurance company: you hand over money (once or over time), and the insurer promises income — starting now or later. In effect you're buying longevity insurance, paying the insurer to take on the risk that you outlive your savings.
  • The simplest and usually best-value kind is a single-premium immediate annuity (SPIA): pay a lump sum, start lifetime income right away. In 2026 a 65-year-old converting $100,000 gets roughly $575–650 a month for life.
  • A deferred income annuity (DIA) pays later; its retirement-account version, the QLAC, lets you move up to $210,000 (2026) of IRA/401(k) money into a longevity annuity that also defers RMDs on that amount until income begins (as late as age 85).
  • More complex products — fixed-indexed and variable annuities — add market-linked returns, caps, and optional riders, along with higher fees and surrender charges. They are heavily sold; know exactly what you're buying.
  • Taxation turns on the source of the money. A qualified annuity (bought with pre-tax IRA/401(k) dollars) pays fully taxable ordinary income. A non-qualified annuity (after-tax dollars) uses an exclusion ratio, so part of each payment is a tax-free return of your own principal.
  • Annuity gains are always ordinary income — never the lower capital-gains rate — and, unlike stocks or a home, annuities get no step-up in basis at death.

The core idea: pooling longevity. Where a pension (Chapter 14) is guaranteed income your employer buys for you, an annuity is guaranteed income you buy for yourself. You give an insurer a lump sum; it pools your money with thousands of other buyers and pays income for as long as you live. Those who die early subsidize those who live long — "mortality credits" — which is exactly why an annuity can pay more than you could safely draw from the same money yourself. It's insurance against the one risk you can't diversify away: living longer than your money lasts.

The main types, simplest to most complex.

- Single-premium immediate annuity (SPIA): pay once, income starts within a year and continues for life. The cleanest, cheapest, easiest-to-compare annuity — pure longevity insurance. You can add a joint-life option (pays until the second spouse dies) or a "period certain" (guarantees payments for, say, 10 years even if you die early), each of which lowers the monthly amount.
- Deferred income annuity (DIA) / longevity annuity: pay now, income starts years later. Because the insurer holds and invests the money longer — and some buyers won't survive to collect — a modest premium today buys a large check later.
- QLAC (Qualified Longevity Annuity Contract): a DIA bought inside a traditional IRA or 401(k). Under SECURE 2.0 you can move up to $210,000 (2026, indexed) into a QLAC, and that money is removed from your RMD base until the annuity starts — which can be as late as age 85. It's a double win: it hedges a very long life and trims the RMDs (Chapter 18) that would otherwise inflate your income in your 70s. A QLAC must be a plain fixed annuity — no variable or indexed features.
- Fixed deferred annuity: like a CD from an insurer — a guaranteed interest rate for a term, tax-deferred until you withdraw.
- Fixed-indexed annuity (FIA): credits a return linked to a market index, but with a cap or participation rate that limits your upside in exchange for a 0% floor (you don't lose principal to market drops). The tradeoffs live in the fine print, and the caps can change.
- Variable annuity (VA): your money goes into market "sub-accounts," so the value rises and falls with investments. VAs often carry the highest costs — mortality-and-expense charges, fund fees, and rider fees can total 2–3%+ a year — sometimes paired with a "guaranteed lifetime withdrawal benefit" rider. Powerful in narrow cases, over-sold in many.

How annuities are taxed. This is where the "qualified vs. non-qualified" split decides everything.

- Qualified annuity (bought with pre-tax money — inside an IRA/401(k), or a rollover): every dollar that comes out is ordinary income, because none of it was ever taxed. These payments also count toward your RMDs (the QLAC exception aside). A subtle but important point: buying an annuity inside an IRA adds no extra tax deferral — the IRA is already tax-deferred — so you should buy it for the income guarantee, never for a tax benefit it can't provide.
- Non-qualified annuity (bought with already-taxed savings): only the growth is ever taxable, and the tax treatment depends on how you take the money. If you annuitize it into a stream, the IRS applies an exclusion ratio — each payment is split into a tax-free return of your principal and a taxable slice of earnings. If instead you take ad-hoc withdrawals from a deferred non-qualified annuity, the rule is LIFO: earnings come out first and are fully taxed as ordinary income (plus a 10% penalty if you're under 59½), and only after all gains are withdrawn do you reach your tax-free principal.

Two tax traps worth naming. First, annuity gains are always ordinary income, never long-term capital gains — so money that might have enjoyed 0/15/20% rates in a brokerage account is taxed at your full rate inside an annuity. Second, annuities get no step-up in basis at death: an heir who inherits a non-qualified annuity owes ordinary income tax on all the built-up gains (though the 10% early-withdrawal penalty doesn't apply to inherited annuities). Contrast that with a taxable brokerage account, whose gains are wiped clean by the step-up (Chapter 25). If leaving a tax-efficient inheritance matters, that's a real mark against annuitizing everything.

Worked example — a SPIA and the exclusion ratio. At 65, John puts $100,000 of after-tax savings into a SPIA paying $600/month ($7,200/year) for life. The IRS tables put his life expectancy near 20 years, so his total expected payments are about $7,200 × 20 = $144,000. The exclusion ratio is $100,000 ÷ $144,000 ≈ 69%, so about $417 of each $600 check is a tax-free return of his own principal and only ~$183 is taxable ordinary income. That favorable split lasts until roughly age 85, when he's recovered his full $100,000 basis — after that, every dollar of each payment is taxable, but the checks keep coming for as long as he lives.

Worked example — a QLAC that defers RMDs. Susan, 73, has $800,000 in a traditional IRA and doesn't need all of her required distributions. She moves $200,000 into a QLAC that will begin paying at 85. That $200,000 immediately drops out of her RMD calculation, shrinking the taxable distributions (and the IRMAA pressure) she faces through her 70s and early 80s. Then at 85 — precisely when she's most at risk of having spent down the rest — the QLAC switches on a large, guaranteed lifetime income. She's used the tax code to buy insurance against her own longevity.

Where annuities fit — and where to be careful. The strongest use is to cover your essential expenses: add up the baseline you must pay every month, subtract Social Security and any pension, and consider annuitizing just enough savings to guarantee the gap. That converts "will my portfolio last?" into "my needs are covered for life," which frees the rest of your money to be invested for growth and flexibility. The cautions are real, though: annuities are illiquid (surrender charges can lock money up for years), most aren't inflation-adjusted unless you buy a costly COLA rider, they carry the insurer's credit risk (state guaranty associations backstop benefits only up to limits that vary by state, often around $250,000), and the complex products carry high fees and strong sales incentives. Favor simple SPIAs and DIAs from highly rated insurers, get quotes from several carriers, consider laddering purchases over time, and annuitize a portion — not everything.

Key action items

  • Size the gap first. Annuitize only enough to cover essential expenses beyond Social Security and any pension — not your whole nest egg.
  • Start simple. Prefer a plain SPIA or DIA; treat fixed-indexed and variable annuities as complex products to scrutinize, not defaults.
  • Use a QLAC to hedge a long life. If large RMDs loom, moving up to the 2026 limit into a QLAC both defers RMDs and insures against outliving your money.
  • Know your tax lane. Understand whether the annuity is qualified (fully taxable) or non-qualified (exclusion ratio), and remember gains are ordinary income with no step-up.
  • Mind liquidity and credit quality. Read the surrender schedule, buy from highly rated insurers, and stay within your state guaranty limits by splitting large amounts across carriers.
  • Shop it like insurance. Get competing quotes, ignore the sales pressure, and skip riders you don't understand or need.

Model it in the calculator

The calculator doesn't sell annuities, but you can approximate one with the inputs it has: cut your starting savings by the premium you'd pay, and add the annuity's payout as "Other taxable income." Run the plan both ways — the full balance invested, versus the smaller balance plus the guaranteed income — and compare how long the money lasts, the tax result, and what's left for heirs. That's the "cover the essentials" trade-off in your own numbers. Just note the tool takes the payout as ordinary income, so it won't capture an annuity's finer tax points — the non-qualified exclusion ratio, or a QLAC's RMD deferral — which you'd weigh separately.
