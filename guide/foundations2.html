Should You Pay Off the Mortgage?

Summary

  • Paying down a mortgage early earns a guaranteed, risk-free return equal to your interest rate. Investing instead might earn more, but the extra return is a risk premium — not a sure thing.
  • The math therefore hinges on your rate. A low rate (say under ~4–5%) tilts toward keeping the mortgage and investing the difference; a higher rate (6%+) makes prepayment a hard-to-beat, low-risk return.
  • Compare apples to apples: the mortgage payoff is a guaranteed return, so weigh it against safe yields (Treasuries, CDs), not the stock market's hoped-for average.
  • The mortgage-interest deduction rarely tips the scales anymore — with the large standard deduction, most households don't itemize, so there's no tax break to "lose" by paying off.
  • The strongest case for paying off isn't mathematical, it's behavioral and structural: a paid-off home slashes your fixed costs, shrinks the income you must generate in retirement, and blunts sequence-of-returns risk — you can ride out a bad market without a mortgage bill to meet.
  • The cost is liquidity: money sunk into home equity is hard to get back without selling or borrowing. Never prepay at the expense of your emergency fund, the employer match, or high-interest debt.
  • Research links homeownership to longer life, but the effect runs through wealth, stability, and lower stress — it's association, not proof that a paid-off mortgage adds years. The peace of mind, though, is real and worth pricing in.
  • For most people the honest answer is "both, in order": invest for the tax-advantaged, higher-expected return while working, and aim to cross into retirement mortgage-free.

The question, stated cleanly. You have extra cash beyond your emergency fund and your match. Should you throw it at the mortgage principal or invest it? Strip away the emotion and it's a comparison of two returns. Prepaying a mortgage "earns" a return exactly equal to your interest rate — every dollar of principal you retire is a dollar you'll never pay interest on again, guaranteed, no market risk. Investing that same dollar has a higher expected return over long horizons but an uncertain one. So the real question is: is the extra expected return from investing worth the risk you take to get it?

Your rate is the fulcrum. Because the mortgage payoff is risk-free, the honest comparison is against other risk-free options — Treasury yields, CDs, high-yield savings — not the stock market's long-run ~7–10%. If your mortgage is at 3%, you can beat it risk-free just by buying Treasuries, and the stock market very likely beats it over decades — keep the cheap loan and invest. If your mortgage is at 7%, paying it off is a guaranteed 7% with zero risk, which is an excellent return in almost any environment and hard to top without taking on real risk. The gray zone in the middle (roughly 4–6%) is where personal factors — taxes, risk tolerance, how close you are to retirement — decide it.

The deduction usually doesn't matter anymore. A common argument for keeping a mortgage is the interest deduction. For most households, that argument is now obsolete. Since the standard deduction was roughly doubled, the large majority of taxpayers don't itemize — which means they get no marginal tax benefit from mortgage interest at all. If you take the standard deduction, paying off the mortgage costs you no tax break, and your effective mortgage rate is simply your stated rate. Only if you itemize (typically higher earners with large mortgages or big charitable giving) does the deduction lower your effective rate and tilt the math slightly toward keeping the loan.

The case for paying it off — mostly not about the math. Even when the spreadsheet narrowly favors investing, three non-math arguments are powerful, especially as you near retirement:

  • It shrinks the number you have to hit. Housing is most people's largest expense. Erase the mortgage and your required retirement income drops by that payment — which lowers the withdrawal rate you need and the taxes you trigger to fund it. A smaller spending target is a safer plan.
  • It blunts sequence risk. A retiree with no mortgage can cut discretionary spending and wait out a bear market. A retiree with a $2,500 monthly payment must sell assets even when they're down — the exact behavior that sequence-of-returns risk punishes. A paid-off home is a form of flexibility.
  • Guaranteed beats hoped-for, and sleep has value. A certain return you can't outlive worry over is worth accepting a slightly lower expected return for. Many people who could "win" by investing still choose the mortgage payoff because the peace of mind is real utility, not a rounding error.

The case against — liquidity and opportunity cost. The dollars you bury in home equity are the least accessible dollars you own. You can't spend a paid-off kitchen; getting the money back means selling the house or taking a new loan (a HELOC or, later, a reverse mortgage) — often at a worse rate and exactly when you least want to. Prepaying also forgoes the tax-advantaged compounding of the 401(k), IRA, and HSA, where a matched or triple-tax-free dollar is worth far more than a dollar of avoided mortgage interest. The firm rule: never prepay ahead of the emergency fund, the full employer match, or any high-interest debt. Those come first, always.

What the "paid-off homeowners live longer" research actually says. There is real, recent evidence linking homeownership to longevity — an Oxford-led study of early-20th-century U.S. men found owners lived modestly longer than renters, and other work reports multi-year gaps in life expectancy between owners and renters. But read it carefully. The studies are about owning versus renting, not specifically about having no mortgage, and the researchers attribute the effect to wealth accumulation, residential stability, stronger community ties, and lower financial stress — not to the deed itself. This is almost certainly correlation, not causation: healthier, higher-income, more stable people are both more likely to own homes and to live longer. So don't pay off your mortgage expecting to buy extra years. Do recognize the honest kernel: financial security and low money-stress are genuinely good for wellbeing, and for many people a mortgage-free home is one of the most reliable ways to feel secure. That's a legitimate reason to value it — just not a medical one.

The pragmatic answer for most people: both, in sequence. You rarely have to choose all-or-nothing. While working, prioritize the tax-advantaged accounts and let a low-rate mortgage ride — the expected-return and tax math favor it. Then, as retirement approaches, redirect surplus toward the mortgage so you arrive debt-free, capturing the cash-flow and sequence-risk benefits when they matter most. If splitting extra cash between investing and prepaying lets you sleep, that's a perfectly rational hedge against being wrong about which return wins.

Worked example

Same $100,000, two different mortgages

Dana has $100,000 of surplus (emergency fund full, no high-interest debt, match captured) and 15 years left on her mortgage. What she should do depends almost entirely on her rate.

Her rateGuaranteed return from payoffRisk-free alternativeSensible lean
2.9%2.9%Treasuries yield more, risk-freeKeep & invest
4.5%4.5%Roughly a wash vs. safe yieldsPersonal call
7.0%7.0%Beating 7% risk-free is hardPay it down

At 2.9%, prepaying locks in a mediocre 2.9% when she can earn more than that with no risk at all — she keeps the loan. At 7.0%, prepaying is a guaranteed 7% with zero risk, which almost nothing safe can match — she pays it down.

The tie-breaker: in the murky middle, weigh the structural benefits. If Dana is five years from retirement, entering it without a payment lowers her required income and lets her ride out a bad market — reasons to pay down even at 4.5%. If she's 30 and decades from retiring, the long runway favors investing the $100,000 in tax-advantaged accounts and revisiting the mortgage later.

Myths & facts

Myth"Paying off the mortgage is always the smart move — debt is bad."
FactNot all debt is equal. A sub-4% mortgage is cheap, tax-advantaged (if you itemize) leverage that decades of market returns typically beat. Reflexively prepaying a low-rate loan can leave you poorer and less liquid than investing would have.
Myth"I'd be crazy to pay it off — the market earns way more than my mortgage rate."
FactThe market's higher return is compensation for risk you're taking; the payoff is a sure thing. Compare the payoff to safe yields, not the market's average. At 6–7% mortgage rates, that guaranteed return is genuinely hard to beat without gambling.
Myth"I have to keep my mortgage for the tax deduction."
FactMost households take the standard deduction and itemize nothing — so they get no tax benefit from mortgage interest, and lose none by paying it off. Check whether you actually itemize before leaning on this argument.
Myth"Studies prove that paying off your mortgage makes you live longer."
FactStudies link home ownership to longer life, but through wealth, stability, and lower stress — it's correlation, not causation, and not about being mortgage-free specifically. The real, defensible benefit is reduced financial stress and security, which is worth pricing in on its own terms.
Myth"I'll drain my savings to kill the mortgage fast, then rebuild."
FactHome equity is illiquid — you can't spend a paid-off wall. Prepaying ahead of your emergency fund or retirement contributions trades flexible, growing, tax-advantaged money for money you can't easily reach. Fund those first, then prepay from true surplus.

Key action items

  • Know your effective rate. If you don't itemize (most people don't), your effective mortgage rate is just your stated rate — compare it against risk-free yields, not the stock market.
  • Let the rate decide the default. Under ~4%: lean toward keeping it and investing. 6%+: prepaying is a strong, low-risk return. In between, let personal factors break the tie.
  • Fund the priorities first. Emergency fund, full employer match, and high-interest debt all come before extra mortgage principal — every time.
  • Weight the structural benefits near retirement. Entering retirement mortgage-free lowers your income target and shields you from sequence risk — reasons to prepay even when the math is a toss-up.
  • Value the peace of mind honestly. A guaranteed return you'll never worry about again is worth a modestly lower expected return to many people. Just don't mistake security for a medical or mathematical certainty.
  • Consider splitting. Dividing surplus between investing and prepaying is a rational hedge when you can't be sure which return wins.
